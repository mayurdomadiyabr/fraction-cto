The rebuild that didn't need to happen

May 20267 min readby the founder

A founder called me on a Tuesday, already half-resigned to spending six figures. Her agency had delivered the verdict with total confidence: the platform was "fundamentally unscalable" and needed a ground-up rebuild. Six months. A team of four. A number with a lot of zeroes. She didn't have the technical background to argue, and the people telling her this were the only technical people she had. So she was going to write the cheque.

We did a two-week Teardown instead. By the end of it, the rebuild was off the table — not because the platform was perfect, but because almost none of the pain was where the agency said it was.

The tell: "fundamentally" is doing a lot of work

When someone reaches for words like fundamental, unscalable, or technical debt without a single number attached, that's not a diagnosis. It's a vibe. Real problems have shapes: this endpoint takes 4 seconds because it makes 60 database calls; this table has no index and it's now 9 million rows; this deploy fails one time in five because two services share a secret nobody rotated.

The agency had none of that. What they had was a codebase they didn't write, a deadline they'd blown, and a strong commercial interest in the most expensive possible answer. A rebuild isn't a fix. It's a way to start the clock over and bill it to you.

What two weeks actually found

I'm not interested in whether code is pretty. I'm interested in what's costing you money, what's about to break, and what's slowing the team down. When you sort the platform by that, the list gets short fast.

  • Three modules were genuinely bad — one of them was the checkout path, which is the only place "bad" was actually hurting revenue.
  • The "unscalable" database was fine. It had two missing indexes. That's an afternoon, not a quarter.
  • The real problem wasn't the code at all. It was that nobody owned it. Three contractors had each built a third of it and left. No one could say why anything worked.

So we refactored the three modules that mattered, added the indexes, and — more importantly — put one accountable person in front of the whole thing. Total cost: a fraction of the rebuild, and the product never went dark for six months while a new version chased the old one's feature list.

The verdict, in one line:
Refactor 3 modules · add 2 indexes · assign 1 owner. Do not rebuild.
— Excerpt from the Teardown report. ~$140k and four months saved, for the cost of one Teardown.

The lesson isn't "agencies lie"

Most don't. The lesson is that an agency takes orders — and "rebuild it" is an order they're happy to take, because no one in the room is paid to say stop, that's the wrong call. That sentence is the entire job. It's the thing a fractional CTO is for and the thing a body shop structurally cannot provide, because it works against their own invoice.

If you're staring at a six-figure rebuild proposal right now and you can't personally pressure-test it, that's not a coding problem. It's a judgment problem — and it's a remarkably cheap one to solve before you sign, versus after.
