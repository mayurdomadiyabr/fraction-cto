Your agency wants your product in its portfolio. Say yes, carefully

Nov 20265 min readby The founder of Fraction

When an agency asks to feature your product in its portfolio or write a case study about it, the safe default is to say "yes, with my written approval of the final version." That keeps the goodwill and the free publicity, while letting you remove anything that reveals your roadmap, your customers, your security setup or details you would not want a competitor or an investor to read. Check your contract first: many agency agreements already include a publicity clause that gives them this right without asking.

It is a reasonable request. Agencies win work by showing work, and a good case study can send referrals your way too. The problem is not the request. The problem is what ends up in the write-up when nobody on your side reads it first.

Check what the contract already says

Before answering, open the master services agreement and search for "publicity", "marketing", "portfolio", "logo" or "reference". You will usually find one of three versions.

A permission clause. The agency may list you as a client and use your name and logo. This is common and mostly harmless, as long as it is limited to the name and logo.

A broad clause. The agency may "describe the services performed" or "display the work product" in its marketing. This one can cover screenshots, architecture descriptions and feature detail. Read it carefully.

An approval clause. Any publicity needs your prior written consent. This is the version you want, and it is a normal request if the contract is still being negotiated. It sits naturally next to the other clauses worth checking in an agency statement of work.

If the contract says nothing, the confidentiality clause usually applies, and most of what would go in a detailed case study is arguably confidential information. Do not rely on that ambiguity in either direction. Agree it in writing.

What can go wrong in a case study

Most case studies are harmless. The ones that cause problems tend to include the same few things.

Your roadmap or strategy

"We are now building the enterprise version with single sign-on and an on-premise option." That sentence tells your competitors where you are going before you have told your customers.

Your customers

Agencies like to show impressive names. If your customers did not agree to be named, a case study that lists them, or shows a screenshot with their data in it, can breach your own customer contracts.

Your security and infrastructure

Architecture diagrams, the exact versions of the frameworks used, how authentication works, which cloud services hold which data. None of this is secret in the sense that it makes you unsafe by itself, but there is no reason to publish a map. Keep descriptions at the level of "a web app and mobile apps on a major cloud provider."

Numbers you have not agreed to share

User counts, revenue, transaction volumes, growth rates. These can end up in front of investors, competitors or future acquirers. If a number appears, it should be one you would happily put in your own pitch deck.

Who actually built what

Some case studies overstate the agency's role, for example implying it designed the product strategy or led the technical architecture when your own team did. That can confuse investors doing diligence, who may read it as a sign the core of the product sits outside the company. I see this come up in technical reviews more than founders expect; it is part of what I look at in a teardown.

A simple approval checklist

When the draft arrives, check five things before approving it.

  1. Names. Is anyone named who has not agreed, including your customers, investors or team members?
  2. Screens. Do screenshots contain real customer data, internal admin pages, or unreleased features?
  3. Numbers. Is every figure one you are comfortable seeing published permanently?
  4. Technical detail. Is the architecture described at a high level, without versions, diagrams of internal systems, or security specifics?
  5. Credit. Does it fairly describe what the agency did and what your team did?

Then ask for one more thing: the right to have it taken down later. Circumstances change. You might be raising, pivoting, or in a dispute with the agency. A short line saying either party can ask for removal on reasonable notice costs nothing to agree now. It is especially worth having if the relationship might end; see planning the agency handoff.

When to say no

There are a few situations where I would simply decline, politely.

  • You are in stealth or have not launched publicly. A case study is a launch announcement you did not schedule.
  • You are mid-fundraise or in acquisition talks. Anything new published about your technology will be read by the other side, and you want to control that story.
  • You work in a regulated area such as health or finance, where customers or regulators expect you to be careful about publicising your systems.
  • The relationship is going badly. Do not let a vendor you may soon replace publish a version of events you disagree with.

In each case, offering a short logo-only listing later, or a quote from you about working with them, often satisfies the agency without the detail.

Make it useful for you too

A case study can work in your favour. If you approve the final text, you can use it yourself: a credible third-party description of how your product was built can reassure enterprise buyers. Ask for a version that links to your site, and ask whether they will share inbound enquiries from companies in your market. That turns a favour into a small, fair exchange.

FAQ

Can an agency put our logo on its website without asking?

It depends on the contract. Many agency agreements include a clause allowing it. If yours does not, and there is no approval clause, it is reasonable to ask them to remove it until you have agreed terms. This is not legal advice; check the wording with a lawyer if it matters.

Should we let them show screenshots?

Only screenshots you have approved, with demo data rather than real customer data, and without internal admin or unreleased screens. A designed mock-up based on the real product is often a better option for both sides.

Is it rude to ask for approval rights?

No. Most established agencies expect it and already have an approval step. Asking calmly, early, and in writing is normal practice. If an agency pushes back hard on approval rights, that tells you something about how it treats confidential information generally.

Does this affect investor diligence?

It can. Investors and acquirers read what is published about your technology and your vendors. A case study that suggests the agency owns or leads your core technology can raise questions about who really owns your code and who can maintain it.
