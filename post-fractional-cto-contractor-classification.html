Is your fractional CTO really a contractor? Check these signs

Nov 20265 min readby The founder of Fraction

Most fractional CTOs are genuine independent contractors, but the contract does not decide that. In the US, the IRS looks at who controls how the work is done, who carries the financial risk, and what the relationship looks like in practice. A fractional CTO who works for several clients, sets their own methods, and invoices through their own business usually passes. One who works fixed hours for you alone, manages your staff day to day, and has no end date starts to look like an employee.

This is not legal or tax advice. Classification rules differ by country and by US state, and the consequences of getting it wrong are expensive. What follows is how I think about it as a practitioner, and what to raise with your accountant or employment lawyer.

Why this matters more for a CTO than for most contractors

A freelance designer who does a logo for you is an easy case. A fractional CTO is harder, because the role is senior, ongoing, and close to the centre of the company. Three features push it toward the line.

It is part of how the company runs

A CTO, even a fractional one, often sits in leadership meetings, sets engineering process, and may manage people. The IRS treats integration into the business and permanency of the relationship as relevant facts.

It often has no end date

Many fractional engagements are open-ended retainers. That is commercially sensible, but an indefinite relationship is one of the signals that points toward employment.

It can slide over time

The engagement that starts as two days a month of advice can become four days a week of hands-on management within a year. The classification question is about what actually happens, not what the contract said at the start.

What the IRS actually looks at

The IRS uses common-law rules and groups the evidence into three categories, set out in IRS Topic 762: behavioral control, financial control, and the relationship of the parties. No single factor decides it, and a contract that calls someone a contractor is not enough on its own.

Behavioral control

Do you have the right to direct how the work is done, not just what result you want? Fixed working hours, required tools and methods, and detailed instructions point toward employment. A fractional CTO who decides how to run an architecture review, when to work on it, and which tools to use is showing independence.

Financial control

Does the person carry their own costs and have a chance of profit or loss? Invoicing through their own company, paying for their own equipment and software, working for multiple clients, and marketing their services to others all point toward contractor status. If you reimburse every expense and they have no other clients, that points the other way. It is also part of why who pays for the tools is worth settling in the contract.

Relationship of the parties

Benefits, permanency, and how central the work is to your business. Offering a fractional CTO health insurance or paid holiday is a strong signal you are treating them as an employee.

The federal and state picture is moving

Federal employment tax uses the IRS common-law test. Wage and hour law under the Fair Labor Standards Act uses a separate "economic reality" test administered by the Department of Labor. That test has changed several times in recent years: a 2024 rule, a 2025 decision to stop enforcing it, and a February 2026 proposal to replace it. Check where it stands when you read this rather than relying on any summary, including mine.

State law can be stricter than either. California's ABC test is the best-known example. A fractional CTO who is clearly a contractor under federal rules can still be an employee under state law, so where you and they are based matters.

If you are in the UK, the equivalent question is IR35, the off-payroll working rules, which have their own tests and their own rules about who makes the status decision. Get UK-specific advice.

Signs the arrangement is drifting

These are the patterns I would treat as a prompt to review the setup with an adviser.

  • The fractional CTO now works most of the week for you and has dropped their other clients.
  • You set their hours, require them in daily standups, and review how they do the work rather than what they deliver.
  • They manage your employees, approve leave, and run performance reviews as if they were a full-time manager.
  • The engagement has no end date, no defined scope, and has run for years.
  • They are paid as an individual rather than through their own business, and you cover all their expenses.

None of these alone makes them an employee. Several together is the point to take advice. Often it is also the point at which the right answer is commercial, not legal: you may have outgrown fractional and need a full-time hire.

How to keep a fractional engagement clean

Most of the protection comes from running the engagement the way a genuine fractional one runs anyway.

Define scope and outcomes

A clear statement of what they are responsible for, measured by outcomes, and reviewed every few months. The guide to scoping a fractional engagement covers how to write one.

Contract with their business

Engage their company or sole proprietorship, receive invoices, and do not offer employee benefits.

Let them run their own practice

Do not require exclusivity or fixed hours. Agree availability and response times instead, which serves the same purpose without directing how they work.

Review when the shape changes

If the role grows into something full-time, have the conversation openly. Converting someone to an employee, or hiring a full-time CTO, is a normal step. The same question comes up with early engineers, covered in when a contractor engineer becomes an employee.

FAQ

Can a fractional CTO be an independent contractor?

Yes, and most are. It depends on the facts: control over how the work is done, financial independence, and the nature of the relationship. Their title does not decide it.

Does a contractor agreement settle the question?

No. The IRS says a contract label is not enough by itself. What happens day to day matters more.

What happens if we get it wrong?

Possible consequences include back payroll taxes, penalties and interest, and wage and benefit claims, depending on the law that applies. That is why it is worth an adviser's time once the arrangement looks like a job.

Should I just make my fractional CTO an employee to be safe?

Only if the role really is a job. If you need a few days a month of senior judgment, a well-run contractor arrangement is appropriate. If you need someone most of the week, that may be a sign to compare fractional and full-time properly.
