Your best engineer candidate can't start for three months

Nov 20266 min readby The founder of Fraction

If your best engineer candidate has a two- or three-month notice period, waiting is usually the right call, as long as they are clearly better than your next option and you use the gap well. Three months feels long in a startup, but restarting a search typically costs more than that, and the person you settle for stays for years. The real risks are that the candidate changes their mind during the notice period, and that you lose momentum while you wait. Both can be managed.

This comes up most when hiring outside the US. Notice periods of 60 to 90 days are common at large IT services firms in India, and contractual notice of one to three months is common across much of Europe. US candidates usually give two weeks, but senior people sometimes ask for longer to hand over properly, and that is a good sign about how they will leave you one day too.

First, compare the wait with the real alternative

Founders tend to compare "three months of waiting" with "someone starting next week". That second option rarely exists. The honest comparison is:

  • Option A: your top candidate, starting in roughly ninety days.
  • Option B: reopen or extend the search, which, for a senior engineer, often takes two to three months before an offer is accepted, and then that person may also have notice to serve.

If option B is a candidate already in your pipeline who is close in quality and can start in two weeks, take that seriously. If option B is "start again", the wait usually wins. I wrote more about how to judge that tradeoff in your engineer search hit three months. Lower the bar?.

Ask about the notice period before the final round

Do not discover the start date after the offer is accepted. Ask early, alongside salary expectations: what is your notice period, is it fixed in your contract, and has your employer released people early before? The answers tell you how firm the date is.

Some candidates can shorten their notice by agreement with their employer, especially if they have unused leave they can offset, or if their manager is reasonable. Others are held to the full period. You cannot negotiate this for them, and you should not pressure them to break their contract.

When a notice buyout makes sense

In some countries, particularly India, it is common for a candidate to "buy out" part of their notice: they pay their current employer for the unserved days, and the new employer reimburses them, often as part of a joining bonus. The usual approach is a pro rata amount of salary for the days not served, but the exact rules depend on the contract and the employer's policy, and the tax treatment differs by country.

I would consider paying for a buyout when:

  • The candidate is clearly your first choice and the role is blocking real work.
  • The amount is a small fraction of first-year cost.
  • Their current employer has a known practice of accepting buyouts, so the money actually buys time.

I would not do it when the employer is likely to refuse anyway, or when the main effect is to push the candidate into a fight with their current company. Structure any reimbursement as a joining bonus with a simple clawback if they leave within a year, and have it written properly. This is not legal or tax advice; check the rules in the candidate's country.

Keep the candidate committed during the gap

The real danger in a long notice period is the counteroffer. Their current employer has three months to make them a better offer, and recruiters keep calling. You lower that risk by staying in contact without being needy:

  • Fix the start date in writing in the offer, and confirm it again a month before.
  • Keep a light, regular touchpoint. A short call every two to three weeks. Share what is happening: a customer win, a design decision, the roadmap they will join.
  • Invite them to one or two team moments if they want, such as a demo or a team lunch. Keep it optional and unpaid work out of it.
  • Do not ask for free work. No "small tasks while you wait". It is unfair, it may breach their current contract, and it can create IP ownership problems for you.
  • Have the counteroffer conversation early. Ask directly: if your employer offers you more money, what will you do? Candidates who have already thought about it are less likely to be surprised into accepting.

If they do take a counteroffer, the post on the senior engineer who said yes and then got a counteroffer covers how to respond. And if they back out entirely, see your engineer backed out after accepting.

Use the three months to prepare, not just to wait

The gap is valuable if you treat it as setup time. Before they start:

  • Write down the first two problems you want them to own. Not a task list, but problems with context: why it matters, what has been tried, what good looks like.
  • Clean up access and documentation. Repository access, cloud accounts, environment setup instructions that actually work, a short architecture overview.
  • Settle the decisions that will be theirs. If they are coming in to set technical direction, do not lock in big choices a month before they arrive.
  • Plan the first week. I covered that in your first engineer starts Monday.

If the work cannot wait three months, bring in short-term help with a narrow scope, such as a contractor for a specific feature, or a fractional technical leader to keep decisions moving. Be clear that the incoming engineer will own the area once they arrive, so they are not inheriting someone else's half-made architecture.

When I would not wait

  • The candidate is good but not clearly better than someone who can start soon.
  • The role is urgent because of a contractual deadline with a customer.
  • You see signs they are still interviewing elsewhere during the notice period and are vague about it.
  • The notice period has already been extended once by their employer, and the date keeps moving.

In those cases, it is reasonable to tell them honestly that the timing does not work and keep the door open for later.

FAQ

Is a three-month notice period normal for software engineers?

It depends on the country. In the US, two weeks is typical. Contractual notice of one to three months is common in much of Europe, and 60 to 90 days is common at many larger employers in India, especially IT services firms.

Should we pay the candidate's notice buyout?

Consider it when the candidate is clearly your first choice, the amount is modest relative to first-year cost, and their employer is known to accept buyouts. Pay it as a joining bonus with a clawback, and check local tax and contract rules.

Can the candidate do some work for us during their notice period?

Avoid it. It can breach their current employment contract, it creates confusion over who owns what they write, and it puts them in a bad position. Keep contact to conversation and context, not work.

How do we reduce the risk of a counteroffer?

Fix the start date in writing, stay in regular light contact, share real progress, and ask them directly early on how they would respond to a counteroffer. Most counteroffer losses happen when nobody talked about the possibility.
