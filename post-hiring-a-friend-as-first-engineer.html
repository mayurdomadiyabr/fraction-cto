Hiring a friend as your first engineer: set the rules first

Nov 20266 min readby The founder of Fraction

Hiring a friend as your first engineer can work well, and it is one of the most common ways early teams form. It works when you run the same process you would run for a stranger, put the same terms in writing, and agree in advance how you will handle it if the job is not working. It fails when the friendship is used as a substitute for any of those three. Trust tells you they will not steal from you. It does not tell you they can build your product.

The reason to be careful is asymmetry. If a stranger does not work out, you lose a hire. If a friend does not work out, you lose a hire and a friend, and you usually wait several months too long to act because of the friendship.

Why founders do it, and why that is reasonable

You already know how they behave under pressure, whether they tell the truth when it is awkward, and whether they finish things. Those are the hardest qualities to assess in an interview. A friend will also often join earlier, for less cash, on less proof, than anyone on the open market.

Those are real advantages. The mistake is letting them answer a different question, which is whether this person is the right engineer for this specific job.

Run the real process anyway

It feels insulting to interview a friend. Do it regardless, and say why: "I want both of us to be sure, and I want to be able to tell investors and future hires that you got the job on merit."

  • Define the job first. Write down what the first engineer must do in the next twelve months before you think about who. If you write the role around your friend, you will get the role your friend can do.
  • Get an independent technical read. You cannot assess a friend's engineering objectively, and if you are non-technical you cannot assess it at all. Have an outside senior engineer or a fractional CTO review their work and talk to them. See who vets your engineer when you have no engineers.
  • Do a paid trial on real work. Two weeks on a real task tells both of you more than any conversation. The structure is in hire your first engineer on a two-week paid trial.
  • Check a reference. One call to someone who has worked with them, not socialized with them. Working with a person and knowing a person are different.

If your friend objects to all of this, treat that as information.

Have the three awkward conversations before the offer

Who decides

At dinner you are equals. At work, someone has the final call on product priorities and, eventually, on whether the role continues. Say out loud who that is. If the honest answer is that your friend expects to be a cofounder with a cofounder's say and equity, that is a different arrangement, and you should negotiate that one instead of drifting into it.

What happens if it is not working

Agree the mechanism now, while nobody is upset. For example: a candid review at 30, 60 and 90 days, either side can end it with a set notice period, and neither of you has to pretend it was something else. It is much easier to use an exit you both designed than to invent one in the middle of a disagreement.

Money and equity

Friends under-negotiate with each other and resent it later. Use market figures for salary and equity as the starting point, then adjust deliberately and write down why. The ranges are in what to pay your first engineer. A "we will sort the equity out later" promise between friends is the single most reliable source of a later falling-out.

Put the standard terms in writing

Every term you would give a stranger, in a signed document:

  • Offer letter with role, salary, start date and who they report to.
  • Equity grant with standard vesting and a cliff. Vesting protects the friendship as much as the company: if it ends at month five, nobody is arguing about what is fair.
  • Intellectual property assignment and confidentiality agreement. Investors will ask for these in diligence, and a missing one from an early engineer is a problem that gets more expensive every month.
  • If they start as a contractor, a written agreement that assigns the work to the company. The trade-offs are in your first engineer does not have to be an employee.

Paperwork between friends feels cold for about a day. Its absence feels much worse for much longer.

Managing a friend day to day

Give feedback at work, in a scheduled one-to-one, in the same words you would use with anyone. Do not deliver it over drinks, and do not soften it into a hint.

Do not discuss company matters with them socially that you would not share with another employee at their level. Once you have a second engineer, perceived favoritism becomes a real cost.

Watch for your own avoidance. The usual failure with a friend is not a blow-up. It is a founder who notices in month three that the work is slow, says nothing until month nine, and has by then lost both the runway and the candor. If the role is not working, the steps are the same as for anyone, and I set them out in your first engineer is not working out. Follow them on the same timeline.

When a friend is the wrong call

In my judgment, pass or restructure if any of these are true:

  • You would not hire them if you had met them for the first time last month.
  • They need the job more than the company needs them, and you both know it.
  • You already suspect you could never let them go.
  • They expect cofounder status and you are offering employment.

In those cases a short paid contract for a defined piece of work is kinder than a full-time role. It lets both of you see the working relationship with a natural end date built in.

FAQ

Should a friend get more equity than a normal first engineer?

Only if they are taking more risk, such as joining before funding or taking well below market salary. Price the risk, not the friendship, and write the reasoning down.

Is it okay to skip the interview if I have seen their work?

If you have actually worked alongside them on a shipped product, a lighter process is reasonable. Still get one independent technical opinion, because you are not a neutral judge.

How do I let a friend go?

Use the exit you agreed at the start, be direct, be generous with notice and references, and do not rewrite history about why. Friendships survive honest endings far more often than slow, avoided ones.

Should my friend report to me or to someone else?

If you have a senior technical lead or a fractional CTO, having your friend report there on technical matters removes a lot of strain. The reporting options are in who your first engineer reports to with a fractional CTO.

If you want a neutral technical read on a friend before you make the offer, book a call. An outside opinion is easier for both of you to hear.
