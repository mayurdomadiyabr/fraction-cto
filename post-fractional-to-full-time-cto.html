Knowing when

Moving from a fractional to a full-time CTO cleanly

Jun 20266 min readby The founder of Fraction

You decided you need a full-time CTO. Good. Now comes the part nobody plans for, which is moving from the fractional or part-time technical leader who got you here to the full-time executive who takes you forward, without dropping the context that made the first arrangement work. I have been on both ends of this handoff, and the difference between a clean one and a messy one is rarely about the people. It is about whether anyone treated the transition as a project instead of an event.

The default failure mode is brutal and common: the founder signs the full-time CTO, thanks the fractional, ends the engagement that week, and assumes the new hire will just pick it up. Three months later the new CTO is still reverse-engineering decisions nobody wrote down, the team has quietly relitigated settled questions, and momentum that took a year to build has leaked away. None of that was necessary.

Treat the overlap as the most valuable month, not a cost

The single highest-leverage thing you can do is build in an overlap. Keep the fractional CTO engaged, usually in a reduced advisory capacity, through the new hire's first four to eight weeks. Founders resist this because it feels like paying twice for the same role. You are not. You are paying once to transfer a year of accumulated context that otherwise walks out the door.

Think about what the fractional CTO actually carries in their head: why the architecture is shaped the way it is, which corners were cut on purpose and which are genuine debt, which engineer is stronger than their title suggests, which vendor relationship is fragile, which past decision the team keeps wanting to reopen and why it should stay closed. Almost none of that is written down, and a new CTO who has to rediscover it by trial and error will spend their first quarter relearning what you already paid to figure out. An overlap converts that tacit knowledge into something the new person can use immediately.

A useful sequence inside that overlap looks like this:

  • The fractional CTO writes the job spec and sits in on final-round interviews, so the person you hire is matched to the real gap rather than a generic title.
  • Before the new CTO starts, the fractional documents the current architecture, the active decisions, the team's strengths and risks, and a candid map of the technical debt.
  • In week one, the fractional walks the new CTO through that map and makes the team introductions personally, transferring trust rather than making the new person earn it cold.
  • Over the following weeks the fractional steps back from decisions and into a sounding-board role, available for the "wait, why is it like this" questions that only surface once someone is in the work.

Hand over artifacts, not just conversations

Verbal handoffs evaporate. What survives is documents the new CTO can return to when you are not in the room. A clean handoff leaves three things behind.

The first is a current-state technical map: the architecture as it actually is, not as the original diagram claims, plus an honest inventory of technical debt with the difference between deliberate shortcuts and accidental mess clearly marked. If you have ever had a senior outsider produce one of these, it looks a lot like the output of a codebase teardown done internally, and it is the single most useful artifact you can hand a new leader.

The second is a working roadmap and a ninety-day plan the new CTO can trust on arrival. Not a plan they are bound to, but a starting point that shows where things stand and what the next decisions are, so they begin from context rather than a blank page. The third is the boring operational layer: who owns what, what the reporting cadence is, where the credentials and the runbooks live, which decisions are already made and should not be reopened. This is also the moment to confirm the unglamorous ownership questions are settled, the same chain-of-title and access issues that matter so much during diligence, so the new CTO inherits a clean situation rather than a hidden mess.

If your original engagement was scoped well, much of this already exists, because a good fractional arrangement produces documentation as it goes rather than at the end. I made that case in scope your fractional CTO before it becomes full-time; a transition is exactly the moment you find out whether that scoping discipline was real.

Align on the timeline long before the handoff

The smoothest transitions I have seen share one trait: the founder and the fractional CTO agreed early that the arrangement was for a season, not forever. When both sides know from the start that the goal is to hand off to a full-time hire eventually, the fractional builds toward that, documents as they go, and treats their own replacement as a success rather than a threat. When it is a surprise, the handoff is rushed and defensive.

So if you are still in the fractional phase and have not had the "what does the end of this look like" conversation, have it now, well before you think you need to. The best fractional CTOs raise it themselves, and many will actively help you run the search and onboard the person who replaces them, because that is the job done right. If you are at the point of planning this transition and want a second set of eyes on whether you are sequencing it well, that is a good thing to talk through on a call before you make the offer, not after the handoff has already gone sideways.

Frequently asked questions

Should the fractional CTO and the new full-time CTO overlap?

Yes, almost always. A four-to-eight-week overlap in a reduced advisory capacity lets the fractional transfer the context, relationships, and decision history that are otherwise lost when the engagement ends abruptly. The cost of paying for that overlap is small next to the cost of a new CTO spending their first quarter rediscovering things you already paid to learn.

What should the fractional CTO hand over before leaving?

Three artifacts: an honest current-state map of the architecture and technical debt, a working roadmap with a ninety-day plan the new hire can trust, and the operational layer covering ownership, reporting cadence, credentials, and which decisions are already settled. Documents survive the transition; verbal walkthroughs alone do not.

Can my fractional CTO help hire their own replacement?

A good one will, and you should ask. They can write an accurate job spec, screen for the gap they know firsthand, and sit in on final interviews, which dramatically lowers the odds of a mis-hire. Treating the fractional as a partner in the search rather than someone being shown the door usually produces a better full-time CTO.

How do I avoid losing momentum during the switch?

Plan the transition as a project with an overlap, documented artifacts, and personal team introductions, rather than as a clean break on the day the new CTO signs. Momentum leaks through rediscovery and relitigated decisions; both are prevented by transferring context deliberately instead of assuming the new hire will absorb it on their own.

When should we start planning the handoff?

Long before you make the offer, ideally from the start of the fractional engagement. Agreeing early that the arrangement is for a season lets the fractional CTO document as they go and build toward a clean exit, which makes the eventual handoff a planned step rather than a scramble. The later you start, the more context you risk losing in the rush.
