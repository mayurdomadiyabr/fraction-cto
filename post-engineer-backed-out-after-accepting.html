Your new engineer backed out before day one

Oct 20266 min readby The founder of Fraction

Your first senior engineer signed the offer letter, and then, a week before their start date, emailed to say they were taking another job. In most cases you have no practical legal recourse and should not look for one. The useful moves are: respond politely, go straight back to your runner-up, and work out what in your process left room for a better offer to arrive. For a small team, the damage is mostly lost time, so the goal is to get that time back.

What a reneged offer actually costs you

For a company with one or two engineers, a backed-out hire hurts in specific ways:

  • Calendar time. A typical senior engineering search takes weeks to months from first post to signed offer. Losing the hire at the finish line can put you back near the start if you closed out the other candidates.
  • Roadmap commitments. If you told investors or customers that a feature would ship once the new person started, that date has moved.
  • Founder focus. The founder who ran the search now has to run it again, usually while also doing the work the new hire was supposed to take over.
  • Team morale. If your existing engineer was counting on help, they will feel it.

Notice what is not on the list: a lawsuit. In the United States, most employment is at will, and a candidate who has not started work can generally walk away. Even where an employment contract exists, pursuing a person who has not started is almost never worth the cost, the delay, or the reputation damage in a small engineering community. Check with your lawyer if your offer letter has unusual terms, but plan as though there is no recourse, because in practice there is none.

The first 48 hours

Reply once, calmly

Thank them for telling you before the start date. Ask, once, whether they would share what made the difference. Some will tell you, and the answer is valuable. Do not argue, guilt-trip, or try to negotiate unless you genuinely believe you misjudged the market. If you want to understand when a single adjustment might make sense, our post on a candidate who took a counteroffer covers that decision.

Call your runner-up the same day

If you had a strong second candidate, call them before you do anything else. Be honest: "We made an offer to someone else and it did not work out. You were a very close second, and we would like to talk about making you an offer." Most people appreciate directness. Some will have moved on; ask anyway.

If you have no runner-up, re-open the role the same day. Repost, message the candidates you liked but did not advance, and tell your network.

Reset expectations internally

Tell your existing team and, if relevant, your board or lead investor, what happened and what the new plan is. A short note is enough. The worst outcome is someone finding out three weeks later that the person they expected never showed up.

Why candidates back out

The reasons are usually predictable once you hear them:

  • They were still interviewing. Many candidates accept the first good offer as insurance while they wait on a preferred company. This is the most common reason.
  • A counteroffer from their current employer. They resigned, their manager made a strong offer to keep them, and they took it.
  • Second thoughts about startup risk. The excitement of the offer faded, and the reality of joining a small company with uncertain funding set in.
  • Something in the gap between offer and start. A long notice period, slow paperwork, or no contact for three weeks lets doubt grow.

Close the gaps in your process

You cannot stop every reneged offer, but you can make them rarer.

Ask about their other processes

During the final round, ask directly: "Where else are you interviewing, and how far along are those processes?" A candidate who is in final rounds at two other companies is a real risk. You do not need to rush them, but you do need to know.

Do not pressure a fast acceptance

Exploding offers feel like they protect you, but they often produce exactly this outcome: the candidate says yes to lock in your offer and keeps looking. A reasonable deadline with a real conversation works better. We have written about setting an engineer offer deadline that is firm without being a trap.

Keep in touch between signature and start

The weeks between accepting and starting are when doubt grows. Invite the new hire to meet the team informally, share the plan for their first month, and give them something real to look forward to. A clear first-month plan, like the one in our first engineer onboarding guide, also tells the candidate that you know what you are doing.

Keep the runner-up warm until day one

Do not send the final "we went another way" note to your second-choice candidate until your hire has actually started, or at least until their notice period is underway and contact has been steady. If you need to tell them something, tell them the truth: you have an offer out, and you would like to stay in touch.

When it keeps happening

One reneged offer is bad luck. Two or three in a row is a signal. The usual causes:

  • Your offer is below market. Candidates accept, then get something better. Check your numbers against current data, not last year's.
  • Your pitch is weaker than your offer. Candidates are excited in the room and less so a week later. That usually means they could not explain clearly to their partner or friends why your company is a good bet.
  • Your process is too slow. If it takes four weeks from final interview to offer, the candidate's other options have time to land.
  • Nobody technical is selling the role. Strong engineers want to talk to someone who can discuss architecture, technical debt, and how decisions get made. A founder without that background can lose candidates without knowing why.

The last one is where a fractional CTO is useful. Someone who can run the technical interviews, answer hard questions about the stack, and tell the candidate honestly what they would be walking into makes the offer more credible. FAQ

Can I sue a candidate who backs out after accepting?

In most at-will employment situations in the US, no practical claim exists, and pursuing one would cost more than it recovers. Talk to a lawyer only if your offer letter included unusual terms, such as a repayable relocation advance.

Should I ask for a signing bonus to be repaid?

If you paid a signing bonus before the start date and the agreement included a repayment clause, you can ask for it back. Most startups avoid this situation by paying signing bonuses on the first payroll, not at signature.

How long should I wait before re-opening the role?

Do not wait. Contact your runner-up the same day, and re-open the search immediately if you do not have one. Waiting to see whether the candidate changes their mind rarely works.

Is a reneged offer a sign I hired badly?

Not necessarily. It usually means the candidate had other options you did not know about. The fix is better information during the process, not a different hiring bar.
