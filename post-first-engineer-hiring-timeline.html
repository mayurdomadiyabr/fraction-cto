How long should hiring your first engineer take?

Sep 20265 min readby The founder of Fraction

Founders ask how long it should take to hire their first engineer, and the honest answer is: much faster than most of them run it. A tight process gets from first contact to offer in about two to three weeks. Left to drift, the same search runs six to ten weeks or longer, and the cost of the drift is not just time. It is that the best candidates, who always have other options, are gone by the time you finish deliberating.

Speed is not the same as haste. You can run a fast loop that still tests judgment properly and still checks references. What you cannot do is run a slow loop and expect to keep strong people warm through it. The teams that win their first hire compress the calendar without lowering the bar, and that takes deciding the shape of the process before you open it, not during.

The target: two to three weeks, not two to three months

A workable first-engineer loop fits in three weeks. Week one is screening and, if you use one, a take-home. Week two is the technical and judgment interviews. Week three is references and the offer. Teams that are truly organized compress this to one or two weeks and catch strong candidates before a larger company has finished its first phone screen.

For context, hiring a software engineer runs roughly six to eleven weeks when a company searches entirely on its own, and three to six weeks with a specialized recruiter, with seniority and the system-design round driving the spread. Those are the averages you are trying to beat, not match. At the first-hire stage, where one person is a large fraction of your team, moving faster than the market is a real advantage, because the candidates you want are the ones the market is also chasing.

The number that matters is not the calendar length in the abstract. It is how long a candidate waits between steps. Every gap is a window for a faster company to make an offer, and top performers do not sit in your pipeline while you think it over.

Why a slow search costs you the best people

The strongest candidates are the ones with the most alternatives, which means they are the first to disappear from a slow process. A search that stretches over months is not being more thorough; it is quietly filtering for the people who had nowhere better to go. That is the opposite of what you want in the person who will set the technical foundation of your company.

Drift also erodes your own conviction. When interviews are spread weeks apart, you forget the texture of earlier candidates, you re-litigate decisions, and you start comparing people to a fading memory instead of to each other. A loop that runs tight keeps every candidate fresh in your mind and makes the comparison honest. This is one reason getting the first senior engineer right depends as much on process discipline as on judging any single person.

There is a real exception. Speed is a virtue only when you have already defined what good looks like. Rushing a search when you cannot describe the ninety-day outcomes, the must-have skills, and how you will test for them does not save time; it just gets you to the wrong hire faster. Do the definition first, then move fast through a process you have already designed.

How to run a fast loop without dropping the bar

Decide the whole process before you talk to anyone. Write down the stages, who runs each one, what signal each is testing for, and the decision rule at the end. A loop you have to invent as you go is a loop that stalls, because every step waits on a decision you have not made yet.

Compress the calendar, not the rigor. Book the technical interview and the judgment conversation in the same week rather than spreading them out. Give feedback within a day of each stage so the next one can be scheduled immediately. Keep the candidate genuinely informed about where they are and what is next, because silence is what loses people who have another offer in hand.

A specialized recruiter can roughly halve the search time by handling sourcing and scheduling, which is often worth it at the first hire when you have no pipeline of your own. Whether that trade is right depends on your budget and network, which is the real question behind using a recruiter for the first hire versus searching direct. Either way, the internal loop still has to be fast, because a recruiter can fill the top of the funnel but cannot make you decide.

The one thing not to compress is the reference and judgment work on your finalist. That is where the expensive mistakes get caught, and skipping it to save two days is how a fast process becomes a fast wrong hire. If you want a technical read on a finalist inside a tight timeline, that is exactly the kind of thing worth a short call about.

FAQ

Isn't a longer process more thorough?

Rarely. Past a point, extra weeks add deliberation, not signal, and they filter out the candidates with other options. A tight three-week loop that tests the right things beats a three-month one that mostly tests your patience. Thoroughness comes from what you test, not how long you take.

What if I cannot find anyone good in three weeks?

Then the problem is usually the top of the funnel, not the calendar. If quality candidates are not appearing, fix sourcing, widen the role, or bring in a recruiter, rather than leaving a slow process open and hoping. A search that stays open for months without strong candidates is a signal to change the approach, not to wait longer.

Should I move fast even for such an important hire?

Yes, once you have defined what good looks like. Speed and rigor are not opposites. Define the outcomes and the tests first, then run those tests quickly. The importance of the hire is an argument for a sharp, well-designed process, not for a slow one.
