Your agency offers a discount for a 12-month commitment

Oct 20266 min readby The founder of Fraction

Your development agency has offered a 10 to 15 percent discount if you sign for 12 months instead of month to month. Take it only if three things are true: you are confident you will need roughly the same team for the whole year, you can exit early for poor delivery without paying the remainder, and the discount is larger than what flexibility is worth to you at your stage. For most pre-seed and seed companies, at least one of those is false.

I get asked about this offer more than almost any other agency term, usually a week before renewal, when the founder is tired and a lower monthly number looks like a win. Here is how I work through it.

What the agency is really buying

A discount is a price. The agency is paying you to take on risk it would otherwise carry. Specifically, it is buying three things:

  • Revenue certainty. An agency with a year of contracted revenue can plan hiring, borrow against it, and stop worrying about your churn.
  • Staffing stability. It can keep the same developers on your account without holding bench time for a possible end.
  • Your switching cost. Once you are committed, your leverage in every later conversation drops, from rate changes to who gets assigned to you.

None of that is sinister. It is reasonable for them to want it. The question is whether you are being paid enough to give it up.

Price the flexibility you are giving away

Start with the money. Say you spend 20,000 dollars a month. A 12 percent discount saves 2,400 dollars a month, or 28,800 dollars over the year. That sounds large.

Now think about what you lose. Early-stage plans change. In the past year, how many of these happened to you or nearly did: a pivot, a raise that took longer than planned, a key feature that turned out to need different skills, a decision to hire in-house, a product that needed half the team for three months? If any one of those happens and you are locked in, the cost of the commitment can wipe out the discount in a single quarter.

A simple test: if you would have wanted to reduce or end the engagement at any point in the last 12 months, you are probably not ready to commit to the next 12. Roughly speaking, if there is a one in three chance you will want to cut the team by half for three months, that alone is worth about as much as the discount above.

Read the exit terms before the price

The discount is not the important number in an annual contract. The exit terms are. Look for these specifically:

Termination for cause

Can you end the contract if the agency misses agreed milestones, swaps key people without approval, or fails on quality? What does "cause" mean, who decides, and is there a cure period? A contract with a 60-day cure period and a vague definition of cause is, in practice, no exit at all.

Termination for convenience

Can you leave for any reason, and what does it cost? A common fair shape is notice plus repayment of the discount you have received so far. That turns the deal into "you got a lower rate while you stayed", which is honest. A clause that makes you pay the full remaining contract value is the one to refuse.

Team composition

Does the contract name the roles and seniority you are paying for, and does the discount survive if they rotate people? An annual commitment with no staffing guarantee lets the agency move its best people to newer clients once you are locked in. We wrote about developer rotation and ramp-up cost separately; an annual deal makes it more likely, not less.

Ramp-down rights

Can you reduce headcount by, say, 25 percent with 30 days' notice without breaking the contract? This single clause is often worth more than the discount itself, and agencies will frequently agree to it if you ask.

When committing is the right call

There are cases where I tell founders to sign:

  • The product is in a stable build phase with a clear roadmap for the next two or three quarters, and the team has already delivered well for six months or more.
  • You have raised, and runway is not the constraint, but predictable cost matters to your board.
  • The agency is giving you something real in return beyond the rate: named senior people, a rate lock against their next increase, or priority staffing.
  • The exit and ramp-down terms are fair, as described above.

If all of that is true, the discount is a sensible reward for a decision you would make anyway.

When to say no, or counter

Say no, or counter, if the offer arrives right after a delivery problem. A discount offered to calm you down after a missed deadline is a retention tactic, and the right response is to fix the delivery issue first. It is also a red flag if the agency pushes for a decision within days, or if the annual price comes with a larger minimum team than you have today.

A good counter-offer usually looks like this: a six-month term instead of twelve, a smaller discount in exchange for ramp-down rights, and a written list of the people on your account. Most agencies accept some version of it, because what they want most is certainty, and six months with stable staffing still gives them a lot of it. If you want a broader playbook for that conversation, see how to cut your agency bill without firing them.

The number to compare against

Before you sign anything, put the annual commitment next to the alternative you might move to within the year. For many companies that is a small in-house team led by part-time senior judgment. The comparison often surprises people; our comparison page and pricing show how a fractional CTO plus one or two hires stacks up against a full agency team. Even if you stay with the agency, knowing that number makes you a better negotiator.

FAQ

What discount is normal for an annual agency commitment?

Offers in the range of 5 to 15 percent are common in my experience. Above that, ask what the agency is getting that makes it worth so much, because it is usually your inability to leave.

Is a 3-month notice period the same as a commitment?

Close to it. A long notice period gives the agency much of the certainty of an annual deal without giving you a discount. If you are on 90 days' notice already, that is leverage: ask for a discount for it, or for a shorter notice period.

Should I sign if my investors like predictable costs?

Predictable cost and locked-in cost are not the same thing. Investors generally care more that you can cut spend quickly if the plan changes. A contract with ramp-down rights gives you both.

Who should review the contract?

A lawyer for the legal terms, and someone technical for the staffing and delivery terms. If you do not have the second, book a call and we will read it with you before you sign.
