How to turn down a finalist engineer without burning them

Nov 20266 min readby The founder of Fraction

When you turn down a finalist engineer, call them within two or three working days, tell them clearly that it is a no, give one or two specific, job-related reasons if they ask, and say honestly whether you would like to stay in touch. Keep anything you write down short and factual. A good rejection costs you fifteen minutes and is one of the cheapest recruiting investments an early startup can make.

Most founders handle this badly, not out of malice but out of discomfort. The finalist spent five or six hours with you across several rounds. You liked them. You chose someone else, or nobody. So the message gets delayed, softened until it means nothing, or sent as a two-line email a week later. In a small engineering market that reputation travels.

Why the finalist rejection matters more at a startup

Your first engineering hires come mostly from networks: former colleagues, friends of your advisors, people who heard about you from someone who interviewed. I covered how much of a first hire comes from that channel in where to find your first senior engineer. Every finalist you reject is inside that network. They will be asked "what were they like?" by someone you want to hire next year.

Finalists are also your best future pipeline. The engineer who came second for your first senior role is often the right person for your second or third. That only works if they left the process feeling respected. I have seen founders go back to their number-two candidate months later, and it worked because the original rejection call was honest and warm. I have seen the opposite too: a strong candidate who would not take a call a year later because they were ghosted after a final round.

How to deliver it

Do it fast and by voice

Aim for two or three working days after the decision. Waiting does not make it kinder; it just extends the candidate's uncertainty and makes your company look disorganised. For anyone who reached the final round, a short phone or video call beats an email. Send a brief written note afterwards to confirm.

Lead with the decision

Open with the answer, not with three minutes of praise that makes them think an offer is coming. Something like: "I wanted to call you myself. We have decided not to move forward with an offer. I know you put a lot of time into this, and I am grateful." Then pause and let them respond.

Give reasons only if they ask, and keep them job-related

Many candidates will ask why. If they do, give one or two specific points tied to the role: "We needed someone who had run a production database migration on their own, and your experience there was more as part of a team." That is useful and defensible.

Avoid anything about personality, energy, "culture fit", age, family, or where they are in life. As Workable's guide to interview feedback points out, vague phrases can be read as a preference that has nothing to do with the job. "We wanted someone with more energy" can sound like "we wanted someone younger". The EEOC's list of prohibited practices is a short read and worth doing before you start interviewing at all.

This is not legal advice. If you are unsure what to say about a particular candidate, ask an employment lawyer before the call, not after.

Say what you actually mean about the future

"We will keep your resume on file" means nothing to a senior engineer. If you would genuinely like to hire them later, say so, say for what, and ask if you can reach out in six months. If you would not, do not pretend. An honest "this is not the right fit for us" is more respectful than a vague promise you will not keep.

What to write down

Your interview notes and the rejection email should be written as if the candidate might one day read them, because in some places they can. Keep notes about evidence from the interview: what they built, what they said about a trade-off, what they could not answer. Do not write impressions about their personality or background.

The written follow-up after the call should be three or four sentences: thank them, confirm the decision, and if you offered, repeat that you would like to stay in touch. Do not put the detailed reasons in writing. The call is where nuance belongs.

The rejections that go wrong

A few patterns I see repeatedly in early searches.

  • The silent no. The founder makes an offer to candidate A and waits to hear back before telling candidate B anything. A takes two weeks. B hears nothing and assumes the worst. If you want B as a backup, tell them honestly that you are in final conversations with another candidate and give a date.
  • The no that is really a "not yet". You liked them but the budget is not there. Say that. "We cannot afford this level right now" is honest, specific, and leaves a door open when you raise.
  • The rejection by a recruiter who never met them. If the candidate spent hours with you, the call should come from you or the hiring manager, not from an agency. I discuss when a recruiter helps and when it hurts in using a recruiter for your first engineer.
  • The feedback essay. A long written critique feels generous to the writer and reads as a performance review to the candidate. Keep it short and spoken.

When nobody got the offer

Sometimes you reject every finalist. That is often the right call, and it usually means the role definition was off rather than the candidates. Before you restart, look at why each finalist fell short. If the reasons are all the same, your bar might be right and your sourcing wrong. If they are all different, your bar is probably unclear. I wrote about this decision in whether to lower the bar after three months.

Tell those finalists the truth too: "We have decided to rethink the role, so we are not making an offer to anyone right now." Engineers respect that more than silence, and some of them will be the right fit once you have rethought it.

FAQ

Should I give feedback to every rejected engineering candidate?

Not to everyone. Early-stage applicants can get a short, polite email. Finalists who spent several hours with you deserve a call and, if they ask, one or two specific job-related reasons.

Is it risky to give a rejected candidate feedback?

It can be if the feedback is vague or touches on personal characteristics. Specific, job-related points delivered by phone carry much less risk than a long written critique. Check with an employment lawyer if a case feels sensitive.

How quickly should I tell a finalist they did not get the job?

Within two or three working days of the decision. If you are waiting on another candidate's answer, tell the finalist that honestly and give a date.

Can I ask a rejected finalist to stay in touch?

Yes, if you mean it. Be specific about the kind of role and rough timing. Do not offer it as a soft landing if you would not actually call them.
