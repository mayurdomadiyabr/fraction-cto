Your engineer candidate asked for more than your range

Nov 20266 min readby The founder of Fraction

When your preferred engineer asks for more than the top of your range, do not answer in the same conversation. First find out what the number is made of: base salary, total compensation they are leaving, or a competing offer. Then decide whether the gap is about the person, the role, or your range being wrong. Move base only if the range itself was wrong, use one-time money for one-time gaps, and be willing to walk away if the answer is "this is simply what I want".

This is the moment where most founder-led engineering searches either close well or create a problem that lasts years. You have run four or five rounds, the candidate is the one you want, and the ask comes in 15 or 20 percent above what you planned. The temptation is to say yes on the spot and worry about it later. Later usually arrives when your second engineer finds out what the first one earns.

First, understand the number

An ask above range is information, not just a demand. Before deciding anything, ask one calm question: "Help me understand how you got to that number."

You will usually hear one of four things.

  • "It is what I make now in total." They are comparing your base to their base plus bonus plus vesting stock. The gap may be real but one-time.
  • "I have another offer at that level." A competing offer. Ask what it is for and where; a staff role at a large company is not the same decision as yours.
  • "It is what I think the role is worth." They have read your scope and priced it higher than you did. Sometimes they are right.
  • "It is my number." No reasoning, just a target. This is the hardest one and the one most likely to repeat at every review.

Do not ask about current salary to verify the claim. In many states that question is now restricted, and I explained why it gives you a bad number anyway in asking a candidate what they earn now. Ask about the move, not the past.

Then diagnose the gap

Your range was wrong

If two or three of your strong candidates have all asked for more, the market is telling you something. Rebuild the range with fresh data: one compensation tool, one founder who hired this level recently, and the asks in your own pipeline. My approach is in what to pay your first engineer. If the new range covers the ask, pay it, and update the range for everyone, including the range in your job post.

The role is bigger than you wrote

Sometimes the candidate is pricing in work you had not admitted the role includes: on-call, managing a contractor, owning infrastructure. If that is true, either pay for the bigger role or shrink it. Do not hire someone at a senior salary for a staff-sized job and expect it to stay stable.

The gap is one-time

If the gap is a bonus due in three months or stock that vests next quarter, a one-time payment solves it without raising the permanent cost of the role. I go through sizing in sign-on bonuses and clawbacks.

The gap is the person

They are exceptional and they know it. Decide honestly whether this engineer is worth more than the role you designed, and whether you can afford the precedent. Sometimes the right answer is to reshape the hire, for example one more senior person instead of two mid-level ones, which is the trade-off I cover in one senior engineer or two mid-level.

What you can move, and what it costs

Founders tend to think in salary only. There are other levers, each with a different cost to your company.

Lever Cost to you When it fits
Higher base Permanent, compounds into every future raise and the next hire Your range was wrong
Sign-on bonus One time, cash now They forfeit a bonus or vesting
More equity Dilution, no cash They value upside and believe in the company
Earlier review A promise you must keep Gap is small and you want evidence first
Scope or title Cheap now, real later The role genuinely is bigger

The table is my rule of thumb from offers I have helped shape, not a market survey.

An earlier review deserves care. "We will revisit at six months" only works if you write down what success looks like and actually hold the meeting. A promise of a future raise that quietly does not happen is the most reliable way I know to lose a senior engineer around month nine.

How to respond

Take a day. Say something like: "Thank you for being direct. I want to give you a considered answer, not a reflex one. Can I come back to you by Thursday?" Then come back by Thursday. If you set a deadline on your own offer, I discuss how to make it reasonable in engineer offer deadlines.

When you reply, give the reasoning, not just the number. "Our range for this level is X to Y, based on these inputs. We can do Y in base and a one-time payment that covers the bonus you would lose." Senior engineers respond well to a transparent method, even when the answer is not everything they asked for.

If the answer is no, say it plainly and leave the door open: "We cannot get to that number this year. If that changes after our raise, I would like to call you." Some candidates will accept a lower base once they see the reasoning. Some will not, and that is a fine outcome too.

When to walk away

Walk away when the gap cannot be explained, when closing it would put this engineer far above anyone you could afford to hire next, or when the negotiation itself has become adversarial. How someone negotiates is a preview of how they will handle disagreements on the team. If the person keeps moving the target after you have met it, they will keep moving it at every review.

If a counteroffer from their current employer arrives in the middle of this, the dynamics change; I cover that separately in when the counteroffer lands.

Getting the first senior engineering offer right shapes the pay of everyone after them. If you would like a second opinion before you reply, book a call.

FAQ

Should I ever pay above my salary range for an engineer?

Only if the range was wrong, and then you should change the range for everyone. Paying one person above it creates a gap you will have to explain to the next hire.

Is a sign-on bonus better than a higher salary?

For a one-time gap, yes, because it does not raise the permanent cost of the role. For an ongoing gap, it just delays the problem to the first anniversary.

Can I offer more equity instead of more salary?

You can, and some engineers prefer it. Make sure they understand what the equity is likely to be worth and how it vests, and do not use it to cover a salary that is below market.

How long should I take to respond to a counter-ask?

A day or two. Long enough to think and check the numbers, short enough that the candidate does not feel ignored.
