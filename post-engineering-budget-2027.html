Pricing the work

Your 2027 engineering budget: the seven lines that matter

Nov 20266 min readby The founder of Fraction

Most seed-stage engineering budgets for 2027 will be wrong in the same places: they count salaries carefully and guess everything else. Build the budget from seven lines, people, leadership, cloud, AI usage, tools, security and compliance, and contingency, and put a named owner and a review month next to each one. The lines founders miss are rarely the big ones. They are the ones that grow quietly with usage.

This is the version I walk founders through each November. It is not an accounting template. It is a way to make sure the number you show your board survives contact with March.

Start with the plan, not last year's spend

Last year's spend tells you what happened, not what you need. Before any numbers, write down three things for 2027: what the product must do by the end of the year, how many customers or users you expect, and whether you plan to raise. Every line below depends on those three answers.

If you skip this, you end up with a budget that is last year plus ten percent, which is how companies keep paying for tools nobody uses and underfund the thing they actually need, usually a senior hire or a security review before an enterprise deal.

The seven lines

People

Salaries, contractors, and agency fees. Count the fully loaded cost, not just salary. Payroll taxes, benefits, equipment and recruiting fees add a meaningful percentage on top of base pay, and the exact figure depends on your country and benefits. Ask your accountant for your real multiplier rather than borrowing someone else's.

Put hiring dates in, not just headcount. A hire planned for January and one planned for September are very different costs for the year. Also budget for the gap: a senior engineer role often takes a few months to fill, and you may pay a contractor in the meantime.

Technical leadership

Whether it is a fractional CTO, an advisor, or a future full-time hire, give it its own line so you can see the decision. If you are unsure what to put here, the guide to budgeting for technical leadership walks through the options, and the pricing page shows what a fractional engagement costs.

Cloud and hosting

This is the line that grows with usage, so budget it per customer or per thousand users, not as a flat number. Look at your bill for the last three months, divide by active customers, and multiply by your plan. Then add a separate amount for things that do not scale with customers: staging environments, backups, logging.

Check your startup cloud credits. Credits that expire in the middle of 2027 create a cliff, and I have watched teams discover it from the invoice.

AI usage

If your product calls a model API, this is a usage cost like cloud, and it needs the same per-customer treatment. Separately, count the tools your engineers use to write code. Both can surprise you, and the second one often sits on individual credit cards. The AI coding tool bill post covers how that one creeps.

Vendor prices in this area change often. Budget from your actual usage and current price, and review it quarterly rather than trusting a number set in November.

Tools and subscriptions

Source control, project management, monitoring, error tracking, design, email delivery, and the rest. Pull a list from your company card statements, not from memory. Every year I do this with a founder we find at least one tool still being paid for after the person who chose it left.

Note the renewal dates. Annual plans that auto-renew in a month when cash is tight are a common, avoidable problem.

Security and compliance

Penetration tests, compliance audits, security tooling, and the time to prepare for them. If an enterprise customer or investor is likely to ask for a security questionnaire, a penetration test, or a formal audit in 2027, the cost is not just the auditor. It is weeks of engineering time. Put both in.

Contingency

Add a contingency line and name it as one. In my experience somewhere between 10 and 20 percent of the non-salary lines is a sensible range for an early-stage team, higher if you are mid-migration or have never run at the scale you are planning for. That is a rule of thumb, not a standard. The point is to make the uncertainty visible instead of hiding it inside optimistic estimates.

The lines founders most often miss

Across the budgets I review, the same items go missing.

Run costs after a build

If an agency is building something for you, the quote usually covers the build, not the hosting, monitoring, app store fees and maintenance after launch. The post on agency quotes and run costs lists what to ask for.

The cost of a hire leaving

Not a happy line, but a real one. At seed stage, losing one engineer can mean months of reduced output plus recruiting costs. You do not need to budget for every departure, but the contingency should be big enough to absorb one.

Time spent on diligence

If you plan to raise, technical diligence takes senior time: documents, calls, a code walkthrough. Budget a few weeks of partial attention from whoever leads engineering.

Make the budget reviewable

A budget is only useful if you look at it. Two habits make that easy.

First, give each line an owner, the person who will notice if it drifts. Second, pick review months. Monthly for cloud and AI usage, quarterly for tools and people, and a full re-forecast mid-year. The goal is to catch a line growing faster than customers before it becomes a board conversation.

When something does drift, ask whether it is growing because of customers, which may be fine, or because of waste, which is not. Those need different responses, and a per-customer cost line makes the difference visible.

A simple sanity check

Once the lines are filled in, compare two numbers: total engineering cost for the year, and what that money must produce. If the plan needs a major product launch, an enterprise-ready security posture and a new platform, and the budget only covers two engineers and a small cloud bill, one of them is wrong. Better to find that out now than in July.

FAQ

How much contingency should a startup engineering budget have?

There is no fixed rule. In my experience 10 to 20 percent of the non-salary lines works for most early-stage teams, more if you are migrating systems or scaling into the unknown. Name it as contingency so it is not spent by default.

Should I budget cloud costs as a fixed amount?

Budget the part that grows with customers per customer, and the part that does not as a fixed amount. A single flat number hides whether growth or waste is driving the bill.

What engineering costs do founders forget most often?

Post-launch run costs, expiring cloud credits, auto-renewing annual tools, AI usage, and the engineering time needed for security reviews and diligence.

Who should own the engineering budget?

The person who leads engineering should own the lines and explain drift. The founder or CEO owns the total and the trade-offs. If you have no technical lead, that is the gap a fractional CTO is often brought in to cover.
