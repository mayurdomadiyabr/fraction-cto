Pricing the work

The rupee fell. Should your India agency's rate come down?

Nov 20267 min readby The founder of Fraction

You pay an agency in India in US dollars. Since late 2025 the rupee has slid from under 89 to the dollar to past 96, a fall of roughly eight percent. Your invoice has not moved. Their costs, which are mostly salaries paid in rupees, have not moved either. So the same dollar invoice now buys them roughly nine percent more rupees than it did.

Is any of that yours? Sometimes, partly. Here is how to think about it, what to ask for, and the version of this conversation that costs you the relationship.

What actually happened to the agency's margin

Start with the arithmetic, because founders usually overestimate it.

Say you pay $20,000 a month for a team of four. At 88 rupees to the dollar that invoice converts to about 17.6 lakh rupees. At 96 it converts to about 19.2 lakh. The agency's rupee revenue from your account went up by about 1.6 lakh a month, roughly $1,700, without anyone doing more work.

Now the parts that eat into that:

  • Salaries did not stand still. Indian IT services firms typically run annual increments, and for engineers with three to six years of experience a high single-digit raise is normal. If the agency gave a round of raises during the same year, a chunk of the currency gain has already gone to the engineers on your project, which is where you want it.
  • They may have hedged. Larger agencies sell forward contracts on their expected dollar receipts. If they locked in a rate months ago, they did not get the full benefit of the fall. Smaller shops rarely hedge, so they got all of it.
  • Their other costs are partly in dollars. Cloud bills, software licenses and some tools are priced in dollars and got more expensive in rupee terms.

A realistic net gain for a small, unhedged agency is somewhere between three and six percent of your invoice. For a larger hedged firm it can be close to zero for the current year. You do not know which one you are dealing with until you ask.

Should you ask for a lower rate?

My default answer is no, not as a straight discount. A few reasons.

First, you agreed a dollar price for a reason: it moved the currency risk onto them. If the rupee had strengthened by eight percent, you would not have volunteered a raise. Asking for the downside to be shared now, when you would have refused the upside, reads as opportunistic, and the account lead will remember it.

Second, a cut on the rate usually comes out of the team, not the agency's margin. The cheapest way for them to absorb a five percent reduction is to rotate your strongest engineer onto a client who pays full price and backfill with someone junior. You save $1,000 a month and lose more than that in output.

Third, exchange rates reverse. The rupee has had long weak stretches before, and partial recoveries. A rate you renegotiate because of one year's move is a rate you will be asked to renegotiate back.

What to ask for instead

There is a better conversation, and it works because it gives the agency a way to share the gain without cutting anyone's pay.

Ask for more capacity, not a lower price. "The currency has moved in your favour this year. Instead of reducing the rate, can we add a part-time QA engineer at the same invoice?" For a $20,000 team, a half-time tester costs the agency far less than the currency gain. You get real output, they keep the revenue line intact, and nobody looks bad.

Ask for a rate freeze at renewal. If your contract has an annual escalator, typically five to ten percent at Indian agencies, the currency move is a strong argument for skipping it this year. That is a request most agencies accept quickly because it costs them nothing they currently have. Our post on handling an agency rate increase mid-project covers how to respond if they push one anyway.

Ask for the senior hours you were promised. Many agency contracts describe a senior architect or tech lead who is supposed to spend a few hours a week on your account and in practice spends none. A good year is the right moment to ask for those hours to actually appear on the timesheet.

Trade it against a longer commitment, carefully. If you were going to sign for twelve months anyway, a currency year is a reasonable moment to ask for a better rate in exchange. But only if you would have committed regardless. Locking in for a discount you did not need is how founders end up stuck with a team they want to leave. The tradeoffs are in whether an annual commitment discount is worth it.

How to raise it without damaging the relationship

Tone matters more than the ask here. Three rules I use.

Raise it at a natural review point, a quarterly review or a renewal, not in the middle of a delivery crunch and never in reply to an invoice.

Lead with the work, not the currency. "We are happy with the team and want to keep going. We also want to talk about how we get more out of the next six months" lands very differently from "the rupee fell, so we want a discount."

Name the currency move plainly, once, and then move to the proposal. Agencies know exactly what the exchange rate did. Pretending you have not noticed is odd. Hammering it is worse.

When a rate cut is fair

There are cases where asking for a lower number is reasonable rather than opportunistic:

  • The rate was set in rupees and converted. If your contract quotes a rupee rate and bills you the dollar equivalent, a weaker rupee already lowers your invoice automatically. Check that it actually did. Some agencies convert at a stale or favourable rate.
  • The team got cheaper. If a senior engineer left and was replaced by someone two levels junior at the same billing rate, the currency is irrelevant. You are owed a rate review on staffing grounds alone.
  • The scope shrank. If you moved from active build to maintenance and the same headcount is billing, the problem is the team size, not the exchange rate. See renegotiating agency spend for that conversation.

A worked example

This is an illustrative composite, not a named client. A seed-stage logistics startup pays a Pune agency $24,000 a month for five people on a dollar contract with a seven percent annual escalator due in January.

A blunt ask would be: "The rupee fell eight percent, cut the rate by eight." The agency would likely counter at three percent and quietly swap the strongest backend engineer.

The better ask: skip the seven percent escalator ($1,680 a month avoided), and add a half-time QA engineer at no change. The founder ends up roughly $20,000 better off over the year than under the original contract, gets testing they did not have, and the agency keeps its best people on the account because the account is still worth what it was.

Questions founders ask

Should I switch my agency contract to rupee billing?

Only if you want to carry currency risk you currently do not. Rupee billing makes your cost fall when the rupee weakens and rise when it strengthens. Most US startups budget in dollars and are better off with a predictable dollar number. If you do switch, agree the reference rate source and conversion date in writing.

My agency says the weak rupee does not help them because of rising salaries. Is that true?

Partly, often. Ask what increments they gave this year and whether they hedge. If raises were around six percent and your dollar now buys nine percent more rupees, they are a few points ahead, not nine. That is useful for a capacity conversation but not a big rate cut.

Does this apply to agencies in other countries?

The logic is the same anywhere the agency's costs are in one currency and your invoice is in another. The size of the gain depends on how far that currency moved against the dollar and how much of the agency's cost base is local salaries.

Is this financial advice?

No. Exchange rates move unpredictably and nothing here is a forecast. The figures above are illustrative, and the rupee rates cited come from market reports of record lows in 2026.
