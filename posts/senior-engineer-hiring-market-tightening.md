If you plan to hire a senior engineer in the next six months, assume it will be harder than it was a year ago. Job postings for software developers have been rising again, and most of that growth is for senior roles, so you are competing with more companies for the same people. The practical answer is to start sooner, run a faster process, decide your pay range from current data, and keep a fallback plan ready in case the search runs long.

## What changed

For most of 2023 and 2024, founders hiring engineers had an easier time. Big companies were cutting staff, and experienced engineers were looking. Many seed-stage founders got used to strong senior candidates replying to a single post.

The data now points the other way. Indeed Hiring Lab figures, [reported by CNBC on 11 October 2026](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/10/11/ai-software-developer-jobs.html), show software development postings up nearly 15 percent since February 2025, even as postings overall have been weak. Senior roles made up about 71 percent of the increase between May 2025 and May 2026, and roles with "AI" in the title made up about 37 percent. Postings are still well below their pre-pandemic level, so this is not a 2021-style frenzy. But the direction has turned, and it has turned specifically at the senior end.

At the same time, entry-level developers are having a harder time. Companies that use AI coding tools heavily seem to want fewer junior engineers and more people who can review, direct, and take responsibility for the work. That is exactly the profile most startups want for their first technical hire.

Layoffs have not stopped either. Several software companies announced cuts in early October 2026. That can make the market look loose from the outside. For the senior engineers you actually want, it often is not: the people who are laid off are not always the people who are hard to find.

## What this means for a founder hiring now

### Your timeline is probably too short

If your plan assumes a senior hire in six weeks, add a buffer. A realistic senior search, from writing the role to the person's first day, often runs two to four months once you include their notice period. With more companies chasing the same candidates, the long end of that range is more likely. Our [first engineer hiring timeline](/post-first-engineer-hiring-timeline) breaks down where the weeks go.

The fix is simple: start earlier. If you need someone by March, the search should be underway now.

### Your process speed matters more

When good candidates have several options, a slow process loses them. The most common delays I see in startup hiring are not in the interviews themselves. They are in the gaps: a week to schedule the next round, another week to decide, a few days to write the offer.

Aim for a decision within a few days of the final interview, and have the offer ready to send. Keep the loop short. For a first senior hire, three or four conversations is usually enough: an intro call, a technical conversation, a practical exercise or work sample, and time with the founders. Our post on [a work sample test that respects the candidate's time](/post-engineer-work-sample-test) explains how to keep the practical step short and useful.

### Counteroffers and second offers become more common

In a tighter market, candidates are more likely to have another offer in hand, and current employers are more likely to make a counteroffer to keep them. Ask early where else the candidate is interviewing. Ask what would make them stay at their current job. If you lose a candidate this way, do not start a bidding war. We covered how to handle that in [your engineer candidate took a counteroffer](/post-engineer-took-counteroffer).

### Your pay range needs current data

The range you set a year ago may now be low, especially for engineers with real AI product experience. Check current compensation data for your location and level before you write the job post, not after you lose your first finalist. If the honest market number does not fit your runway, it is better to know that now than to discover it after a month of interviews. Our [senior engineer cost and runway](/post-senior-engineer-cost-runway) post walks through the trade-offs.

## Where to look when the market is tight

Job posts alone will bring you lots of applicants and relatively few strong senior ones. In a competitive market, the best candidates usually come through other routes:

- **Your network and your investors' networks.** Ask specifically: "Who is the best engineer you have worked with who might want to join an early-stage company?" Specific questions get better answers than "do you know anyone?"
- **People who recently left companies you respect.** Layoffs and reorganizations release experienced people. Some of them are good fits for a startup, especially those who liked being close to the product.
- **Former founders.** Engineers who have run their own company often understand startup trade-offs better than anyone. There are trade-offs to this profile; we covered them in [hiring a former founder as an engineer](/post-hire-former-founder-engineer).
- **Strong mid-level engineers ready to step up.** Not every first hire needs ten years of experience. A strong engineer with four or five years and clear judgment can grow into the role, especially with senior guidance around them.

## Decide what senior actually means for you

When the market tightens, founders tend to raise the bar to protect themselves, then wonder why nobody qualifies. Be specific about what you need.

For most seed-stage companies, a senior first engineer needs to:

- Ship features end to end without close supervision.
- Make sensible architecture decisions and explain them.
- Review AI-generated code critically rather than accepting it.
- Tell you, honestly, when a plan is going to take longer or cost more than you think.

They usually do not need deep experience with your exact stack, experience at a famous company, or prior management experience. Every requirement you drop that is not truly necessary widens the pool you are hiring from. Our guide to [screening engineers for judgment](/post-screen-engineer-for-judgment) shows how to test for the things that matter.

## Have a plan B ready

Even with a good process, a senior search can stall. Decide in advance what you will do if you have not hired by a specific date.

### Option 1: Hire one level down, with guidance

A strong mid-level engineer paired with experienced technical guidance can cover a lot of ground. The guidance is what makes it work: someone to review the architecture, catch the risky decisions, and help the engineer grow.

### Option 2: Bring in senior judgment part-time

If the bottleneck is technical leadership rather than hands on keyboards, a fractional CTO can make the key decisions, review the work, and help you hire while the search continues. This is often the cheapest way to keep a product moving through a long search. [Fraction's pricing](/pricing) shows how that kind of engagement is structured.

### Option 3: Narrow the scope

If you cannot hire the engineer you need, reduce what the product needs to do in the meantime. A smaller, well-built scope with the people you have beats a larger plan waiting on a hire that has not happened.

## What not to do

- **Do not panic-hire.** A wrong senior hire costs far more than a few extra weeks of searching.
- **Do not lower the bar on judgment.** You can be flexible on experience and stack. You cannot be flexible on whether the person makes sound decisions and tells you the truth.
- **Do not sell a role you have not defined.** In a competitive market, strong candidates will ask hard questions about the problem, the architecture, and how decisions get made. Have clear answers.

## FAQ

### Is it harder to hire senior engineers in late 2026?

The data suggests it is getting harder. Software development postings have been rising since early 2025, with most of the growth in senior roles, so more companies are competing for experienced engineers. Exact difficulty still depends on your location, budget, and stack.

### How long does it take to hire a senior engineer at a startup?

Plan for two to four months from opening the role to the person's first day, including their notice period. Faster is possible through warm referrals; longer is common if the process is slow or the pay range is below market.

### Should I hire a junior engineer instead because they are easier to find?

Usually not as your first or only engineer. Junior engineers need direction and review that a non-technical founder cannot provide. A junior hire can work well later, alongside a senior engineer or with experienced technical guidance.

### What can I do if my senior search stalls?

Have a fallback ready: hire a strong mid-level engineer with senior guidance, bring in a fractional CTO to cover technical leadership, or narrow the product scope until the hire lands.
