Posting a job and waiting is the default move, and it is also the reason most first-engineer searches stall. A public post to a general board optimizes for volume, and volume is the opposite of what you want. The senior engineer who can build your product from nothing is almost never scanning job boards, because they already have a good job and a full inbox. You are not running a search. You are trying to reach specific people who are not looking, and that requires different channels than the ones that produce a hundred unqualified applications.

I have helped founders fill this exact seat, and the pattern is consistent. The hires that work come through warm paths and builder communities. The applications that flood in from a cold post are mostly a tax on your time. Here is where the people you actually want tend to be, roughly in order of signal.

## Start with founder referrals

The single best channel is other founders. Ask people one stage ahead of you who their first engineer was, whether they would hire them again, and who that engineer respects. This works because the recommendation carries judgment you do not have yet: another founder has already watched this person build under startup conditions, which is the exact thing you cannot assess from a resume. It also reaches passive candidates, because a warm introduction from a mutual contact gets a reply that a cold message never will.

If you do not have a founder network yet, build the smallest possible version of one. Investors, even ones who passed, will make introductions. Advisors will. The founder of a company you admire will often take one email. You are not asking them to vouch for you, you are asking who they would talk to. Most people answer that question generously.

## Go where builders already are

The second tier is communities of people who build things for the love of it. Open-source projects are full of engineers shipping real, reviewable work in public, which is a far better signal than any interview, because you can read months of their decisions before you ever talk. Hackathons, demo days, and early-stage builder events attract exactly the personality that thrives in a zero-to-one role: people who like fast execution and making something from nothing. Alumni networks of companies known for engineering, the Stripes and Airbnbs and Notions of the world, produce people who learned what good looks like inside a strong team and now want the smaller-team energy again.

The common thread is that these people are already demonstrating the trait you care about most, which is that they build without being told to. Someone maintaining an open-source library on nights and weekends has shown you their motivation in a way no cover letter can. Sourcing from these communities is slower than posting a job, and it is slower in the way that saves you a mis-hire.

## Use the startup-specific platforms, skip the general ones

If you are going to post, post where startup-minded engineers actually look. Wellfound, formerly AngelList Talent, is built for this and its candidates are self-selecting for startup roles. That is a meaningfully different pool from a general job board, where most applicants want stability and a big-company brand, which is fine for them and wrong for a founding role. Recruiting firms that specialize in early-stage and founding-engineer searches, especially the ones that work a referral network to reach passive people, can earn their fee for a first hire where trust and warm intros matter more than volume.

Before you spend on any of that, settle a cheaper question: does this first hire even need to be a full-time employee? I have argued that [your first engineer does not have to be an employee](/post-contractor-vs-employee-first-engineer), and starting with a contract engagement can be a better way to reach senior people who are not ready to leave their job for an unproven company, while also functioning as a long, honest trial for both sides.

## Sell the role, do not just screen for it

Here is the part founders skip. When you are reaching senior engineers who are not looking, your job is not to filter applicants, it is to make someone want a thing they were not seeking. Founding engineers weigh the mission, the equity, and you as a founder as heavily as the cash, and often more. A clear, specific story about what you are building and why it matters will out-recruit a bigger salary from a company with a boring pitch. The earliest companies routinely land excellent engineers on narrative alone, because the narrative is the only thing they have that a later-stage company does not.

That means every sourcing conversation is half evaluation and half sales, and you have to be genuinely good at the sales half. If you cannot explain in two sentences why a talented engineer should bet a year of their career on you, fix that before you start reaching out, because no channel compensates for a pitch that does not land. Once you have candidates, the harder problem becomes de-risking the bet, which I have written about in [how to stop guessing on your first senior engineer](/post-first-hire).

## What to skip

Skip broad, general-purpose job boards for a first senior hire. Skip long unpaid application gauntlets, which drive off exactly the people with options. Skip the instinct to wait passively after posting, because the people you want are not going to find you. The work of a first-engineer search is outbound and relational, and it is more like fundraising than like filling a role. ### FAQ

### How long should finding a first senior engineer take?
Plan for one to three months of active outbound, not passive waiting. The timeline stretches when founders treat it as a job posting and shrinks when they treat it as a relationship-driven search with real weekly effort behind it.

### I have no network at all. Where do I actually start?
Start with investors and advisors for introductions, then open-source communities and Wellfound for reach. Every warm introduction you land creates the next one, so the network compounds once you begin, but you have to make the first uncomfortable asks.

### Should I use a recruiter for my first engineer?
A specialist early-stage recruiter can help, particularly one who works a referral network to reach passive candidates, since a founding hire is about trust and warm intros more than volume. General contingency recruiters optimized for filling seats fast are usually a poor fit for this specific role.

### Is it worth paying a premium salary to close a senior engineer early?
Cash matters, but for founding engineers mission, equity, and belief in the founder usually matter more. A compelling, specific story about the company frequently beats a higher offer from a duller opportunity, so invest in the pitch before you assume you have to outbid.
