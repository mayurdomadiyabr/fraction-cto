If your first engineer will work from a different US state than your company, you will usually need to register as an employer in their state before their first payday. That means a state withholding account if the state has income tax, a state unemployment insurance account, workers' compensation coverage that includes that state, and a new-hire report. A payroll provider can do most of the filing, but it cannot pick the state for you, and it will not catch every local rule.

Founders often find this out on the first payroll run, when the software refuses to process because the state account number is missing. It is cheap to get right in advance and annoying to fix afterwards.

## Why the employee's state matters, not yours

Employment obligations mostly follow where the person physically works, not where the company is incorporated or headquartered. A Delaware company with an office in New York that hires an engineer who works from home in Colorado is, for payroll and most employment law, a Colorado employer for that person.

That covers more than tax. The employee's state usually sets the rules on minimum salary for overtime exemptions, paid sick leave, final paycheck timing, required notices, and what can go into the offer letter. A clause that is fine in your state may be unenforceable in theirs. Non-competes are the usual example: several states restrict or ban them for employees.

## The setup checklist, in order

### 1. Confirm where they will actually work

Ask, and write it down. "Remote" is not a state. If they split time between two states, or plan to move in six months, say so now, because each state they work from can create obligations.

### 2. Register for state income tax withholding

Most states with an income tax require an employer account before you withhold. A few states have no wage income tax, which removes this step but not the others. Registration is usually online with the state revenue department and can take anywhere from a same-day number to a few weeks, so start as soon as the offer is signed.

### 3. Register for state unemployment insurance

Unemployment tax generally goes to one state per employee, normally the state where the work is done. You will get a new-employer rate and an account number. Your payroll provider needs this number to run payroll.

### 4. Extend workers' compensation

Workers' comp is required for employees in almost every state, and your current policy may only cover your home state. Tell your insurer or payroll-bundled carrier, and get the new state added. A few states run their own state fund, which changes how you buy coverage.

### 5. File the new-hire report

Federal law requires employers to report new hires to the state, generally within 20 days of hire, and some states set shorter deadlines. Many payroll providers file this automatically once the state accounts are in place; confirm that yours does.

### 6. Check local taxes and rules

Some cities and counties have their own payroll taxes or employment rules. If your engineer lives in one of them, there may be another registration.

### 7. Paperwork and notices

Collect the federal W-4 and any state withholding form, complete the I-9 as usual, and provide the state's required notices and posters. For remote employees these are usually delivered electronically. Some states also require a written notice of pay rate and payday at hire.

## Do you need to register the company itself in that state?

Separate from payroll, a company that does business in a state may need to register as a foreign entity there, which can bring an annual fee and franchise or income tax filings. Whether one remote employee triggers that depends on the state and what the person does. An engineer writing code at home is treated differently from a salesperson closing deals locally, but the rules vary. This is a question for your accountant, and the answer should be in writing.

## What it costs and how long it takes

The money is modest. Registrations are usually free or close to it; the state unemployment tax for a new employer is a small percentage of a capped wage base; workers' comp for a software engineer is low because the role is low-risk. A payroll provider with multi-state support may charge a little more per state.

The time is the real cost. Plan for one to three weeks to get every account number, longer in a slow state. If the engineer starts before you are registered, you either delay their first pay or fix filings after the fact, and neither is a good first impression on your first hire. If you have not yet decided whether this person should be remote at all, read [should your first engineer work remote or onsite](/post-first-engineer-remote-or-onsite) first.

## FAQ

### Do I need to register in another state for one remote employee?

In most cases, yes. Payroll tax and unemployment obligations generally follow where the employee works, so even a single remote employee in another state usually means employer registration there.

### Which state do I withhold income tax for?

Usually the state where the employee physically works. A small number of states apply a "convenience of the employer" rule that can tax remote days as if worked at your office in that state, so check if your company is based in one of them.

### Can my payroll provider handle all of this?

Most modern payroll providers can file registrations and new-hire reports for you, often for an extra fee. They still need you to tell them the correct work state, and they may not handle foreign entity registration or local rules.

### What if the engineer moves to another state later?

Treat it like a new hire for payroll: register in the new state, update withholding and unemployment, extend workers' comp, and close the old accounts once final filings are done. Ask employees to tell you before they move, not after.
