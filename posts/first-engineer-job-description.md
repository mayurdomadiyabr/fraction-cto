Most first-engineer job posts read like they were copied from a company ten times the size. A wall of required technologies, five years of this framework and three of that one, a list of responsibilities that describes a cog in a machine that does not exist yet. Then the founder wonders why the applications are all wrong: mid-level people who match the keyword list and none of the people who could actually build a company from nothing. The job post is doing the wrong job.

A first-engineer post is not a filter for skills. It is a filter for a kind of person, and it is a sales document aimed at people who are not looking. The engineer you want has a good job and a full inbox. They are not scanning boards for a stack that matches their resume. They are occasionally, quietly, wondering whether there is something more interesting out there. Your post is either the thing that makes them lean in or the thing that confirms you do not understand the role you are hiring for.

## Describe the reality, not a wish list

The strongest thing you can do in a first-engineer post is tell the truth about the job, including the parts most companies hide. The founding engineer builds the front end, the back end, the database, the deploys, and sometimes the design. They sit with the founders and decide what to build next. They talk to users, because the product has to find a market or the company dies. That is the actual job. Say it.

If you are pre-product-market fit, say so. If the roadmap is unstable, say so. If the role includes customer calls, recruiting the next hires, and the cleanup work nobody else will do, say so. This feels risky, like you are talking people out of applying. You are, and that is the point. The person who reads that the roadmap will change every two weeks and gets excited to own that ambiguity is exactly the person you want. The person who needs a stable spec and clear tickets will be miserable in a first-engineering seat, and you want them to self-select out before they cost you a hire.

### Lead with ownership, not with your tech stack

A founding-engineer post should emphasize extreme ownership, comfort with ambiguity, and zero-to-one building, not a technology checklist. The specific frameworks matter far less than most founders think. A strong generalist learns your stack in a few weeks; nobody learns product judgment or the temperament for chaos in a few weeks. If you over-index on the tech list, you filter for the wrong axis and you scare off the [generalist who is exactly the right first hire](/post-generalist-first-engineer).

So lead with the impact. This person will choose the initial architecture, build the first real version of the product, set the engineering culture, and shape what the company becomes technically. Then describe the qualities that predict success in that seat: product intuition, technical judgment, user empathy, and a willingness to operate without the support layers a big company provides. Put the technologies at the bottom as context, framed as here is what we use today rather than here is what you must have mastered.

## Be specific about the reward, including the numbers

Vague compensation language reads as either a lowball or a lack of seriousness, and the best candidates skip both. You do not have to publish an exact figure, but you should anchor the range honestly. US founding-engineer base salary in 2026 runs roughly $140,000 to $240,000 depending on stage and location. The first engineer typically receives a median equity grant around 1.5%, and for very early or unusually senior hires that can reach 2 to 4%.

Naming a real range does two things. It filters out people whose expectations you cannot meet before you both waste three interviews. And it signals that you have actually thought about what this role is worth, which is itself a trust signal to someone deciding whether you are a serious operator. If you are still working out what the number should be, the tradeoffs are worth getting right first, and I have written separately about [how to set your first engineer's salary and equity](/post-first-engineer-compensation).

## Write it like a person, to a person

The last thing that separates a post that pulls from one that dies is voice. Corporate boilerplate is invisible to the exact people you want, because they read a hundred of those a month and have learned to skim right past them. A first-engineer post that sounds like a founder talking honestly about a hard, interesting problem stands out precisely because so few do.

Say what the company is trying to do and why it matters. Say what is genuinely hard about it right now. Say what kind of person tends to thrive here and what kind does not. A great post can be shared as a link in a warm intro and do half your selling for you, because it already answers the question the candidate is really asking, which is not what stack do you use but would I regret leaving my comfortable job for this. Remember that the post is only the top of the funnel; the people worth hiring mostly arrive through referrals and builder communities, so write it to be forwardable, and pair it with active outreach to [where your first senior engineer actually is](/post-find-first-senior-engineer).

## Frequently asked questions

### Should I list required technologies in a first-engineer job post?
Keep them minimal and at the bottom, framed as what you use today rather than a hard requirement. A strong generalist learns your stack quickly; over-weighting the tech list filters for the wrong quality and drives off the best fits.

### Should I put the salary and equity in the post?
Put at least an honest range. Founding-engineer base pay in 2026 runs roughly $140,000 to $240,000 with a median equity grant near 1.5%. Naming a real range filters mismatched expectations early and signals you are a serious operator.

### Is it a mistake to describe the hard parts of the job?
No, it is the point. Saying the roadmap is unstable and the role includes customer calls and cleanup work makes the wrong candidates self-select out and the right ones lean in. Honesty is a feature in a first-engineer post.

### My job post is getting lots of applicants but none are right. Why?
Usually the post reads like a big-company role and optimizes for keyword-matchers instead of builders. Lead with ownership and impact, describe the real job, and treat the post as a forwardable sales document. If you want a read on your version, [book a call](/book-a-call).
