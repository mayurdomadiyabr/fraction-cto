If you are a non-technical founder with a budget for a first version, you will end up choosing between two very different kinds of help. One is a freelance developer: one person, often cheaper per hour, who you manage directly. The other is an agency: a team with a project manager, a designer and a process, at a higher blended rate. Most advice frames this as a price comparison. It is really a question about who does the management work, and whether you can do it.

The right choice depends less on budget than on three things: how clear your scope is, how much of the coordination you can carry yourself, and what happens to the product after version one ships.

## What you are actually buying

An agency sells you a delivery system. You get engineering, but you also get someone whose job is to turn your fuzzy idea into tasks, sequence them, chase the people doing them, and tell you when something is late. You also get design, testing and some continuity: if one developer leaves, the agency assigns another.

A freelancer sells you engineering capacity. A good one is often more senior than the developers an agency puts on a small account, and you talk to the person writing the code instead of a go-between. What you do not get is the system around them. Scoping, prioritising, testing, design and chasing are now your job, or they do not happen.

This is why the hourly rates are not comparable. An agency rate includes coordination you would otherwise be doing yourself, unpaid, in evenings. A freelancer rate that is half the agency rate is not half the cost if you have to become a part-time product manager to use it well.

## When a freelancer is the better choice

A freelancer tends to work best when:

- The scope is small and well defined: a prototype to test demand, an internal tool, an integration between two systems you already use
- You can write clear requirements and judge whether a feature does what you asked
- The product is mostly one kind of work, for example a web app with a standard backend, rather than web, mobile, design and infrastructure all at once
- You have a technical advisor who can review the work occasionally, because you cannot tell good code from bad code yourself

The risk with a freelancer is concentration. Everything you know about the product lives in one person's head and one person's calendar. If they take a full-time job, get sick, or pick up a bigger client, your roadmap stops. Guard against that from day one: the code lives in a repository your company owns, the hosting is in your name, and you ask for a short written note on how to run and deploy the thing. [Why you need repo access while they build](/post-agency-repo-access-during-build) applies to freelancers just as much as agencies.

## When an agency is the better choice

An agency tends to work best when:

- The first version spans several disciplines at once, typically design plus a web app plus a mobile app
- You do not have the time or experience to manage developers day to day
- A deadline is fixed by something outside your control, such as a launch event or a pilot contract, and you need the capacity to add people
- You need testing and a release process from the start, for example because customers will pay from day one

The risks here are the usual agency risks: a senior team at the pitch and a junior team on the work, fixed bids that grow with every change request, and code that only the agency understands. None of these are reasons to avoid agencies. They are reasons to structure the contract well. [Picking between fixed bid, dedicated team and staff augmentation](/post-agency-engagement-model) matters as much as picking the agency, and it is worth reading [the SOW clauses that cost you later](/post-sow-red-flags) before you sign.

## The question most founders skip: what happens after launch

Version one is not the end of the spending. It is the start of a product that needs bug fixes, small changes and eventually a real team. Think about which path makes that easier.

A freelancer-built product can be easy to hand to your first full-time engineer, if the freelancer kept things simple and documented. It can also be a one-off tangle that only its author understands. An agency-built product is usually more conventional in structure, but you may find the agency wants a monthly maintenance retainer to keep touching it, and your first hire may inherit a codebase built for the agency's convenience rather than yours.

Either way, the protection is the same: own the accounts, own the repository, insist on plain conventional technology rather than something exotic, and plan the handoff before you need it.

## A middle path that often works

Plenty of founders do not have to pick just one. A common, sensible setup is:

- A freelance designer to shape the product and the screens
- A freelance senior developer, or a very small agency, to build it
- A few hours a month of senior technical oversight to review scope, check the code and catch expensive mistakes early

The oversight piece is what most founders skip and most regret. It is the difference between finding out in week three that the database choice will not scale and finding out in month nine. ## FAQ

### Is a freelancer always cheaper than an agency?

Per hour, usually. Per finished product, not always. Once you count the management time you contribute, the risk of the one person disappearing, and the rework from unclear scope, the gap narrows and sometimes reverses.

### How do I evaluate a freelancer when I cannot read code?

Look at products they built that are live and that you can use, speak to two previous clients about reliability and communication, and pay for a small, time-boxed first task before the big one. If you can, have a technical person review that first task.

### Should I pay a freelancer hourly or per milestone?

For a well-defined build, paying per milestone with clear acceptance criteria keeps both sides honest. For ongoing or exploratory work, hourly with a weekly cap is easier. [Milestone billing or retainer](/post-milestone-billing-or-retainer) covers the tradeoffs in more detail.
