You found the right senior engineer. They aced the work sample, the references were real, they said the role sounded great and they were excited. You sent the offer. Then their current employer countered, and now the person you spent two months finding is thinking it over. This is not bad luck. In 2026 it is the base case, and founders who treat the offer as the end of the process keep losing candidates in the last mile.

Counter-offers have become the default. Roughly 60% of senior engineering offers now get countered, and for specialized roles or people at well-funded companies that figure climbs to 80 or 90%. If you are hiring a strong first engineer, you should assume the counter is coming and build your process so it does not surprise you. The founders who close consistently are not paying the most. They are managing the offer as its own stage, with the same care they gave the interviews.

## Diagnose the counter risk in week one, not at the offer

The mistake is waiting until the offer is out to think about competing offers. By then you have no time and no information. The fix is to surface the risk during the first real conversation.

Ask, early and plainly, why they are open to leaving. Get the reasons in specific terms and remember them. Someone whose only stated reason is money is a high counter-offer risk, because money is the one thing their current employer can match in an afternoon. Someone who is leaving because the work stopped growing, or they are tired of the politics, or they want to build something from nothing, is a much safer bet, because a raise does not fix any of that. This matters because money alone rarely tells the whole story: in exit research, 63% of people who quit cited no opportunity for advancement and 57% cited feeling disrespected, not pay.

So your job during the process is to learn what this specific person actually wants and then speak to it. A first-engineer role at a startup can offer things a big company structurally cannot: real ownership, direct impact on the product, a seat at the decisions. If those are the reasons they are talking to you, name them, because those are the reasons a counter-offer cannot touch.

### Name the counter before it lands

Late in the process, say it out loud: when you resign, there is a good chance they come back with more money and a nice speech about your future there. Ask what they think they will do. This does two things. It removes the ambush, because the moment is no longer a surprise. And it gets them to rehearse their own reasons for leaving while they are still clear-headed, before they are sitting across from a manager applying pressure.

## Move fast and stay close after yes

Counter-offers thrive in the gap between a soft yes and a signed document. Close that gap. Get a verbal yes converted to a written offer inside 24 hours. Ambiguity is where a candidate's resolve leaks out, and every day of it is a day their current employer can work on them.

A few mechanics that measurably help offers hold:

- Use a meaningful signing bonus paid on day one, not deferred to day ninety. It signals real commitment from you and creates a small switching cost for them.
- Set the start date two to three weeks out, not four to six. Long notice periods are counter-offer incubators, because they hand the other side a month of access.
- During the notice period, stay in contact yourself. A founder text every two or three days keeps the relationship warm. Corporate radio silence is exactly the vacuum where candidates get talked out of leaving.

None of this is about outbidding anyone. It is about not leaving your candidate alone in the most pressured two weeks of the process.

## If the counter lands anyway, get on the phone

If your candidate calls to say they got a counter, do not answer by email. Call them within the hour, because right now they are in a hard conversation with someone who does not want to lose them, and a written reply cannot compete with that. On the phone, do not re-sell the salary. Take them back to the reasons they gave you for leaving in the first place, the ones a raise does not solve, and ask whether those reasons changed today or just got papered over with money.

It also helps to know the data, and to share it gently. About 40% of people who accept their employer's counter-offer leave within a year anyway, because the underlying reasons never went away, they just went quiet. A candidate who understands that they are one raise away from being right back in this exact search is a candidate thinking clearly again.

And if you lose them, lose them well. Stay warm. Some of the best hires I have seen came from a candidate who took the counter, spent nine unhappy months confirming they were right the first time, and came back. Before you get to any of this, of course, you have to be reaching the right people at all, which starts with knowing [where your first senior engineer actually is](/post-find-first-senior-engineer). And the offer only closes if the compensation is sane for the stage, which is its own decision worth getting right on [what to pay your first engineer in salary and equity](/post-first-engineer-compensation).

## Frequently asked questions

### How likely is my engineering candidate to get a counter-offer?
Very. About 60% of senior engineering offers are countered, rising to 80 to 90% for well-funded companies and specialized roles. Assume it will happen and plan for it rather than hoping it will not.

### Should I just offer more money to prevent a counter-offer?
Rarely the right lever alone. Money is the one thing the current employer can match instantly. Compete on what a startup uniquely offers, ownership, impact, and a real seat at the table, and reserve pay for making the base offer fair, not for a bidding war.

### How fast should I turn a verbal yes into a written offer?
Within 24 hours. The window between a verbal acceptance and a signed document is exactly where counter-offers do their work. Speed and a day-one signing bonus close it.

### My candidate accepted a counter-offer. Is it over?
Not necessarily. Around 40% of people who accept a counter leave within a year because the original reasons persist. Stay in friendly contact. If you want a second opinion on your whole hiring process, [book a call](/book-a-call).
