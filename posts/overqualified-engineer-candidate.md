An overqualified engineer is usually not a problem, and often the best hire available to a small company. The label hides three separate questions: will they actually do the hands-on work, can you afford them without distorting your pay structure, and will they stay long enough to matter. If the answers are yes, enough, and probably, hire them. Rejecting someone for having too much experience, without asking why they want the job, throws away the cheapest source of senior judgment a startup can get.

It is also worth knowing that "overqualified" used as a reason to turn down an older candidate can amount to age discrimination. In the United States the federal age discrimination law protects workers aged 40 and over at employers above a size threshold, and many state laws reach smaller companies. This is not legal advice, but it is a good reason to decide on the three questions below and write those down, rather than the label.

## Why senior people apply for smaller jobs

Ask directly. The common reasons are ordinary and most are good news for you.

- **They want to build again.** Years of management meetings, and they miss writing software. These people are often excellent early engineers.
- **They want a smaller company.** They have seen what a large organization does to the pace of work.
- **Life changed.** They moved, want remote work, want fewer hours, or are done with a long commute.
- **They were laid off.** Senior roles are fewer than senior people. This is a reason for the search, not a comment on ability.
- **They care about your specific problem.** The rarest and the best.

The reason that should worry you is the unstated one: this is a stopgap until a director role appears. You find that out by asking what they would do if a bigger title came along in six months, and by listening to how specific the answer is.

## Test one: will they do the hands-on work

This is the real risk, and it has nothing to do with attitude. Someone who has managed for eight years may not have shipped code alone in a long time. At a startup there is no platform team, no designer on call, no one to delegate to.

Ask for evidence, not assurance:

- When did you last ship something end to end yourself, including deployment? What was it?
- What would you do in your first two weeks here, concretely?
- Which parts of this job are you not looking forward to?

Then use a paid work sample on a real task, exactly as you would for any candidate. The format is in [the take-home test stopped working](/post-engineer-work-sample-test). A senior person who is serious about hands-on work will not be offended by it. One who is offended has answered the question.

The specific failure pattern for people coming from large companies, such as designing for scale you do not have and waiting for specifications nobody will write, is covered in [hiring a senior engineer who only worked at big companies](/post-senior-engineer-from-big-company).

## Test two: can you afford them properly

Do not assume you cannot. Ask what they need. Many senior candidates applying to a seed-stage company have already accepted a pay cut and want to know whether the equity and the work make up for it.

What you should not do is invent a special deal. Set the band for the role, pay at the top of it if their experience justifies that, and use equity for the rest. A salary far outside your structure becomes a problem the day you hire the second engineer. The band logic is in [what to pay your first engineer](/post-first-engineer-compensation), and the runway arithmetic in [a senior engineer costs a fifth of your seed round](/post-senior-engineer-cost-runway).

If the gap is too wide, consider fewer hours rather than a lower rate. Four days a week from a very senior engineer is frequently worth more than five from a mid-level one.

## Test three: will they stay

Nobody can promise this, and you should not ask them to. You can improve the odds.

- **Give them real scope.** Senior people leave when the job is smaller than they were told. If they will own architecture, hiring input and technical decisions, say so, and mean it.
- **Be honest about the boring parts.** Fixing a billing bug, answering a support ticket, writing the migration by hand. Say it in the interview.
- **Use standard vesting.** A four-year schedule with a one-year cliff already prices in the risk of a short stay. You do not need extra clauses.
- **Agree what growth looks like.** If the company grows, do they want to lead the team or stay an individual contributor? Either is fine. A mismatch on this is the usual cause of a departure at eighteen months, which I wrote about in [your first engineer will get bored around month 18](/post-keep-first-engineer).

Even a two-year stay from a strong senior engineer can be a good outcome if they leave behind a sound architecture, written decisions and a team that can run without them.

## The title question

An overqualified candidate will sometimes ask for a title that matches their history: head of engineering, VP, CTO. Do not hand it over as a sweetener. A title is a promise about scope and about who you can hire above them later. Decide it on what the role is now and what you would be comfortable with at twenty people. The trade-offs are in [senior, staff, head of: titles for your first engineers](/post-engineer-titles-early-team).

## When to pass

In my judgment, these are legitimate reasons, and each is about the job rather than the person's age or seniority:

- They could not show recent hands-on work and declined a work sample.
- They want to manage a team you do not have and will not have for a year.
- Their pay requirement cannot be met inside your structure, even with reduced hours.
- They described the role as temporary in their own words.

Write the actual reason down. "Overqualified" is not one.

## FAQ

### Is it risky to hire someone more experienced than the founders?

It is a benefit if you are willing to be challenged. You get better decisions and you will be told when you are wrong. How to manage that relationship is in [can you manage an engineer more technical than you](/post-manage-engineer-more-technical).

### Will an overqualified engineer get bored?

They will if the work is narrow. Give ownership of outcomes rather than a queue of tickets, and bring them into decisions about customers and priorities.

### Should I offer a senior candidate less because they are between jobs?

No. Pay for the role and the value. A discount extracted from someone's circumstances is recovered by them leaving as soon as the market improves.

### Could a fractional arrangement work instead?

Sometimes. If what you need is senior judgment a few days a month rather than full-time building, a fractional setup may fit better. The [comparison page](/comparison) sets out the options side by side.
