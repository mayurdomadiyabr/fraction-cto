A sign-on bonus with a clawback can work for an early-stage company, but only if you treat it as a bridge for something specific the candidate is giving up, keep it small relative to salary, and write the repayment terms so a court and the candidate would both call them fair. Use it to close a gap you can name, such as an unvested bonus at their current job. Do not use it as a cheaper substitute for the salary you should be paying, and do not lean on the clawback to keep someone who wants to leave.

I get asked about this most often in the last week of a senior engineer search. The candidate likes the company, the salary is a little under their current package, and the founder wants one more lever that does not raise the monthly burn. A one-time bonus looks like that lever. Sometimes it is. Often it causes more trouble than the gap it was meant to close.

## When a sign-on bonus is the right tool

A sign-on bonus solves a one-time problem. The candidate is walking away from money that would have arrived if they stayed: an annual bonus due in three months, a retention payment, or a tranche of stock that vests next quarter. Your offer cannot match that loss through salary without raising the cost of the role forever.

In that situation a one-time payment is honest. It says "we know you are losing X by leaving now, and we will cover it." It is also cheap compared to the alternative. A $15,000 bonus costs you $15,000 once. Adding $15,000 to base salary costs you that every year, plus whatever it does to the pay of the next engineer you hire, who will eventually find out.

### When it is the wrong tool

It is the wrong tool when the real gap is ongoing. If your salary is below market and the candidate's concern is what they earn in year two, a bonus does nothing for year two. They will feel the gap again on the first anniversary, and that is when engineers start taking recruiter calls. I covered how to set the base number itself in [what to pay your first engineer](/post-first-engineer-compensation); fix that first.

It is also the wrong tool when you are trying to win against a counteroffer from the current employer. A bonus rarely beats a counteroffer, because the counteroffer is usually a raise. If you are in that fight, read [what to do when the counteroffer lands](/post-win-senior-engineer-offer) before adding cash.

## How big the bonus should be

My rule of thumb for a seed-stage company: size the bonus to the specific thing being forfeited, and cap it around 10 to 15 percent of first-year base salary. That is judgment from the offers I have helped shape, not a published benchmark. Above that range, a bonus starts to distort your cash plan and signals that the salary is wrong.

Do the arithmetic the candidate is doing. If they are leaving a $20,000 annual bonus that pays out in two months, offering $8,000 will not move them, and offering $30,000 overpays for a problem you can measure. Ask them what they are forfeiting and when it would have paid. Most candidates will tell you plainly, and the conversation itself builds trust.

### Paying it in pieces

Splitting the bonus is often better than one lump sum. Half on the first payroll and half at six months reduces your exposure if the hire does not work out, and it removes most of the need for a clawback at all. If the second half is simply not paid when someone leaves before six months, there is nothing to recover and nothing to argue about. Check how your payroll provider and local law treat a promised but unpaid future installment before you write it this way.

## The clawback: what it is and where it goes wrong

A clawback, sometimes called a stay-or-pay term, says the engineer repays some or all of the bonus if they leave within a set period. Founders like them because they feel like insurance. In practice they rarely pay out, and they can cost more than they recover.

Three things go wrong most often:

- **You never collect.** Asking a departed employee to write you a check is awkward, slow, and sometimes requires a lawyer. For a $10,000 bonus, the legal fees can exceed what you recover.
- **The term poisons the exit.** An engineer who is leaving anyway now has a financial grievance with you, during the exact weeks when you need a clean handover.
- **The term itself is restricted.** Several jurisdictions now limit these agreements, and the rules are moving.

### The legal ground is shifting

This is not legal advice, and you should have employment counsel review any repayment clause before it goes into an offer. But you should know the direction of travel before you talk to them.

California is the clearest example. A 2025 law, AB 692, restricted "stay-or-pay" terms in employment contracts. In September 2026 a follow-up bill, AB 1697, pushed its application to contracts entered into on or after January 1, 2027, and widened some exceptions. According to [Foley and Lardner's summary of the amended law](https://www.foley.com/insights/publications/2026/10/a-reprieve-not-a-reversal-california-revises-its-stay-or-pay-law/), a discretionary bonus can still carry a repayment term only if all of these hold:

- The repayment terms sit in a separate agreement from the main employment contract.
- The engineer is told they may consult a lawyer and gets at least five business days to do so before signing.
- Repayment is interest-free and prorated to the time left in a retention period of no more than two years.
- The engineer can choose to defer the bonus until the retention period ends and owe nothing.
- Repayment is triggered only by voluntary resignation or a termination for misconduct, not by a layoff.

Violations make the term unenforceable and expose the employer to damages of at least $5,000 per worker plus attorney fees, per the same summary. Other states have their own rules, and the federal picture on these agreements has changed more than once in recent years. If your engineer lives in a different state from your company, the rules where they work usually matter more. I wrote about that setup in [hiring an engineer in another state](/post-first-engineer-lives-in-another-state).

Even where a clawback is allowed, the California list is a good template for a fair one anywhere: short period, prorated, no interest, nothing owed if you let them go.

## A structure that works for most early teams

Here is what I usually suggest to founders, starting from the simplest option:

1. **No clawback, split payment.** Pay half at start and half at six months, with the second half conditional on still being employed. Cleanest for everyone.
2. **Short, prorated clawback.** If you must pay up front, a 12-month period with monthly proration. Leave at month four and repay two-thirds. No repayment if you end the employment without cause.
3. **Separate, plain document.** One page, written in plain English, signed separately from the offer letter, with time to review.

Whatever you pick, say it out loud in the offer call. An engineer who first discovers a repayment clause in the paperwork will wonder what else you did not mention.

## What a bonus will not fix

A sign-on bonus closes a one-time gap. It does not fix a weak offer, a slow process, or a role that is unclear. If you find yourself adding a bonus to every senior offer, the problem is upstream: your salary band, your equity story, or how long your hiring takes. ## FAQ

### Is a sign-on bonus taxed differently from salary?

In the United States a sign-on bonus is generally treated as supplemental wages and is subject to withholding through payroll like other pay. The withholding method can differ from regular salary, so the net amount may look different in the first paycheck. Your payroll provider handles the mechanics; tell the candidate to expect it.

### Can I ask for the bonus back if the engineer is fired?

You should not, in most cases. Recovering a bonus after you end someone's employment without misconduct is unfair and is specifically barred in some states, including under California's amended rules for contracts from 2027. Limit repayment to voluntary resignation or misconduct.

### Is equity a better tool than a sign-on bonus?

For an ongoing gap, often yes, because equity rewards staying. For a one-time loss like a forfeited bonus, cash matches the problem better. Many offers use a small amount of each.

### How long should the clawback period be?

Twelve months is enough for most early-stage hires and keeps the term easy to defend. Two years is the outer limit under California's amended rules, and longer than most small companies need.
