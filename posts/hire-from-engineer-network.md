Your first engineer is great, and they know three other great engineers. Hiring from their network feels like the obvious move. It is fast, the trust is built in, and referred candidates often perform well.

The short answer: referrals are usually the best source for your second and third engineers, but a team built entirely from one person's network has predictable blind spots. Use referrals deliberately, run the same bar on referred candidates as on everyone else, and watch for the moment the team starts to think like one person.

## Why referrals work so well early

At a small startup, the hardest thing to screen for is not skill. It is whether someone can work with little direction, deal with a messy codebase, and stay through a rough quarter. A person who has worked with the candidate before has seen all of that. No interview loop you can design will match two years of shared on-call rotations.

Referrals also move faster. The candidate already trusts the person referring them, so they take the first call, believe what they hear about the company, and accept faster. For a seed-stage company competing with larger employers, that trust often decides the offer. The rest of that problem is covered in [how to win a senior engineer's offer](/post-win-senior-engineer-offer).

## The risks nobody mentions

### You hire one worldview three times

If your first engineer and their two former colleagues all came from the same company, they share its habits: the same stack preferences, the same testing culture, the same blind spots. That feels like fast agreement. Six months later you notice that nobody on the team ever argues with an architecture decision, and that every problem gets solved the way their old employer solved it.

### Your first engineer becomes a gatekeeper

When most of the team came through one person, that person gains informal authority over hiring, promotions, and technical direction, whether or not they want it. That is fine if they are the person you intend to lead the team. It is a problem if you later hire an engineering lead from outside, who inherits a team whose loyalty sits with someone else. The timing for that hire is covered in [when to hire an engineering lead](/post-when-to-hire-engineering-lead).

### The bar quietly drops

Founders often skip steps for a referred candidate: one friendly call, no work sample, no reference checks from anyone outside the friendship. Then the referred hire underperforms and nobody wants to say it, because saying it means criticizing a colleague's judgment. The social cost of a bad referral is higher than the cost of a bad cold hire, which is exactly why the bar should not move.

### Departures travel in groups

People who followed one person into a company tend to follow them out. If your first engineer leaves, expect some of the people they brought to reconsider too.

## How to use referrals without the downside

### Run the same process for everyone

Referred candidates get the same interview structure, the same work sample, and the same reference checks as anyone else. Tell the referring engineer this up front so it does not feel like distrust. A structured [work sample test](/post-engineer-work-sample-test) is the easiest way to make the comparison fair.

### Ask the referrer the right question

"Are they good?" gets you a yes. Better questions: "What would they be worst at here?" "What did people complain about when working with them?" "Would you hire them if you had to manage them, not just work next to them?" The answers are the most valuable reference you will get.

### Keep the referrer out of the final decision

The referrer should share what they know and can help sell the company. They should not sit on the interview panel or vote. That protects them socially if the answer is no, and it protects your process.

### Set a mix target

A simple rule that works: no more than about half of the first five engineers from one person's network. It is a rough guide rather than a law, but it forces you to run at least some searches through other channels, which you will need anyway once the network runs dry.

### Pay a referral bonus, but keep it simple

A modest bonus paid after the new hire has been with you for a few months is fair and common. Paying on the start date rewards introductions, not good hires.

## Where the other half comes from

The channels that work for early engineering hires, besides referrals: your own network and investors' networks, focused job posts in communities where the stack you use is discussed, a good recruiter for senior roles (the tradeoffs are in [should you use a recruiter for your first engineer](/post-recruiter-first-engineer-hire)), and former contractors who already know the codebase.

## A composite example

A Series A logistics startup hired its first engineer from a large ride-sharing company. Over nine months, three more engineers joined, all former colleagues. Velocity was excellent. Then the team proposed moving to a microservices setup that matched their old employer's, for a product with a few hundred customers. Nobody on the team questioned it, because everyone had worked that way before. An outside technical review caught the problem before the migration started, and the next two hires came from different backgrounds on purpose. The team ended up faster, not slower, because decisions got argued properly. The general pattern behind that migration is in [monolith or microservices](/post-monolith-or-microservices).

## Where a fractional CTO helps

The job of an outside technical leader here is simple: be the person with no social stake in the referral. A fractional CTO can run interviews, calibrate the bar, and point out when the team is converging on one worldview. If you are about to make several hires from one network and want an outside view, you can [book a call](/book-a-call).

## FAQ

### Are referred engineers actually better hires?

Often, because of the shared history and better information on both sides. But the advantage comes from that information, not from the referral itself. Skip the checks and you lose it.

### Should I let my first engineer hire their former team?

One or two, yes, if they meet the bar. A whole team, rarely. Leave room for people who will disagree with them.

### How big should a referral bonus be?

It varies by market. Pick an amount that feels meaningful but not distorting, and pay it after a few months of the new hire's tenure rather than on day one.

### What if a referred hire is not working out?

Handle it the same way as any other hire, with direct feedback and a clear timeline. Tell the referrer privately and early, before it becomes a surprise.
