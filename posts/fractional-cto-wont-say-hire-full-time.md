A good fractional CTO should, at some point, work themselves out of a job. The engagement is supposed to end, either because you elevated someone internal or because you hired a full-time leader who takes the seat for good. That is the healthy version.

The problem is that the person best positioned to tell you the moment has arrived is the same person who loses a retainer when they say it. I am a fractional CTO. I have sat in this exact spot, and I want to be honest about the incentive, because most founders never see it.

## The conflict nobody names

The math is simple and uncomfortable. A fractional CTO on a two-day-a-week retainer is billing you somewhere between $6,000 and $15,000 a month in 2026. The day I tell you "you have outgrown me, hire a full-time head of engineering," that revenue stops. Not reduced. Stops.

Most fractional operators are honest and will make the call anyway, because their reputation depends on the companies they leave being better off. But the incentive is real, and it does not require anyone to be a villain to bend the outcome. It just requires a little inertia. The engagement drifts. The fractional CTO keeps showing up, the work keeps feeling useful, and the conversation about "so when do we transition off you" quietly never gets scheduled. Six months later you are paying for part-time strategic leadership when what the company actually needs is a full-time person in the room every day.

This is the flip side of a post I wrote about [the signs you have outgrown your fractional CTO](/post-outgrown-fractional-cto). That one is about the symptoms. This one is about why the person who should flag those symptoms sometimes doesn't.

## Read the signal yourself

Because you cannot fully rely on the incumbent to raise the flag, you need your own tripwires. Here are the ones that actually mean it is time, drawn from engagements I have watched end well and a few that dragged.

### The work has become management, not architecture

A fractional CTO is priced and scoped for judgment: architecture calls, hiring decisions, vendor pushback, the occasional fire. When the bulk of the week has quietly become managing people, running one-on-ones, unblocking three engineers a day, mediating between teammates, you are paying a strategic day rate for a job that needs a full-time manager present. A fractional leader cannot manage a growing team from two days a week and outside the building. If your fractional CTO is spending most of their time on people rather than decisions, the model has already broken.

### You are hiring three or more engineers this quarter

There is a specific inflection the research on failed fractional engagements keeps surfacing: the founder who cannot personally fill the people-leadership gap and is bringing on multiple engineers in a short window needs a full-time leader, because onboarding, standards, and culture cannot be set part-time from outside. If you have three or more hires landing in the next quarter, start the full-time search now, not after they arrive and flounder.

### Decisions are waiting for the two days

If engineering keeps stalling on the days your fractional CTO is not in, that is not a scheduling problem to solve with better async docs. It is the company telling you the technical seat needs to be occupied full-time. I wrote about the founder version of this in [when every technical decision waits on you](/post-non-technical-founder-bottleneck); the same bottleneck can form around a part-time leader.

### You just raised, and the runway changes the math

Before a round, a fractional CTO at $10,000 a month instead of a full-timer at $400,000 fully loaded a year is obviously the right call. After a Series A, with a real engineering budget and a two-to-three-year plan, the calculus flips. The cost that made fractional a bargain at pre-seed is a rounding error against a funded roadmap, and continuity of a full-time leader is worth more than the savings.

## How to force the conversation

The fix is structural, and you set it up at the start, not the end. Three things keep the incentive honest.

Put an off-ramp in the engagement from day one. Agree, in writing, on the conditions that mean the engagement converts or ends: headcount over a threshold, a funding event, the team hitting a size where daily leadership is required. When the trigger is written down, nobody has to be the bad guy later; the contract already made the call. This is part of what I mean by [scoping the engagement before it becomes open-ended](/post-scoping-fractional-engagement).

Ask the direct question at every quarterly review: "If you were me, would you still be paying for fractional, or would you hire full-time now?" A good operator will answer straight even against their own interest. How they answer tells you a lot about whether to keep them for the transition.

Watch what they do when the honest answer is "hire full-time." The fractional CTOs worth keeping through a transition are the ones who help you write the job description, interview candidates, and hand off cleanly. If yours gets vague when the topic comes up, you already have your answer.

## The reframe

A fractional CTO is a bridge, not a destination. The best ones tell you when you have reached the far side, even though it costs them the fare. But you should never outsource that judgment entirely to the person being paid to stay. Keep your own tripwires, write the off-ramp into the deal, and treat the transition as a success to plan for rather than a loss to avoid. ## FAQ

### Is my fractional CTO ripping me off if they haven't raised this?
Not necessarily. Most are honest and simply have not scheduled the conversation, because nothing forces it. The point is not that they are dishonest, it is that you should not rely solely on the incumbent to flag the moment. Keep your own tripwires.

### What is the clearest single sign it is time to go full-time?
The work has shifted from judgment to daily people management. A fractional leader is scoped for decisions, not for managing a growing team from outside the building two days a week.

### Should the same person become my full-time CTO?
Sometimes, but treat it as a real hiring decision, not a default. The skills that make a great fractional advisor are not identical to running a full-time team. Interview them the way you would any candidate.

### How do I avoid this trap from the start?
Write an off-ramp into the engagement: the specific headcount, funding, or team-size triggers that mean the role converts or ends. When the condition is in the contract, the transition happens on schedule instead of drifting.
