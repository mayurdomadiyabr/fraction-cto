Expect one free conversation from a fractional CTO, long enough for both sides to decide whether to work together, and not much more. A good first call should leave you with a clear view of your problem and whether they are the right person for it. It should not leave you with a free architecture review, a hiring plan, or a vendor decision. If you need those before you commit, pay for a short, scoped piece of work instead.

I take a lot of first calls. The best ones are useful to the founder whether or not we end up working together. The worst ones are either a sales pitch with no substance, or a founder trying to get three months of advice in an hour. Both waste time.

## What a free first call is for

The call has two jobs, and they are the same for both sides.

### Deciding whether the problem fits

You want to know whether this person understands your kind of problem. They want to know whether it is something they can help with. A seed-stage marketplace with an unhappy agency relationship is a different problem from a Series A company preparing for diligence, and not every fractional CTO is good at both.

### Deciding whether you can work together

How they ask questions, whether they listen, and whether they are honest about what they do not know. You learn more from how someone handles "I am not sure we need you" than from any slide.

## What it is reasonable to get for free

In an hour, a good fractional CTO should be able to give you a few things without charging.

### A plain restatement of the problem

Often the most useful part of the call. Founders describe symptoms, slow releases or an expensive agency, and a good advisor names the likely cause, or at least the two or three possible causes.

### An honest view of whether you need them

Sometimes the answer is that you do not need a fractional CTO yet. A senior engineer, a [one-off technical review](/post-technical-review-cost), or a better agency contract may solve it. If an advisor never says this to anyone, be wary.

### A rough shape and price

How many days a month they think it would take, what they would do first, and roughly what it costs. If they cannot give you a range, ask why. [How many days a month a fractional CTO needs](/post-how-many-days-fractional-cto) gives a sense of what is normal.

## What you should expect to pay for

Anything that requires them to look at your systems, read your documents, or produce something you will keep.

### Reviewing code, architecture, or contracts

Reading a codebase or an agency statement of work properly takes hours. It is real work and should be priced as such, even if the call ends with an informal impression.

### Written recommendations

A memo, a plan, a hiring scorecard, or a vendor comparison is a deliverable. If someone offers that for free as part of a pitch, ask how much care went into it, and whether it is generic.

### Repeated calls

A second free call to clarify a proposal is fine. A third, fourth and fifth, where you are really getting ongoing advice, is a fractional engagement without a contract. Most advisors will eventually stop taking those calls, and they are right to.

## Why "free strategy sessions" can cost you more

Some firms offer long free workshops or audits. They are not always a bad deal, but understand what they are.

### They are a sales tool

A free audit is designed to find problems that the firm then offers to fix. That does not make the findings wrong, but it means the incentive is to find work. Ask who would do the follow-on work and whether the audit's author benefits from it.

### They tend to be generic

Unpaid work gets less time. A free two-hour "assessment" usually produces a checklist that would apply to most startups. A paid two-day review produces something about your company.

### They blur the relationship

If you take a lot of free help, it can become awkward to say no, or to hire someone else. Paying for scoped work keeps both sides free to walk away.

## A better option: a short paid start

If you need more than one call before committing to a retainer, pay for a small, fixed piece of work. Typical shapes are a short review of the codebase and team, a few hours on a specific decision, or a [paid trial month](/post-fractional-cto-paid-trial) with a clear goal.

This is good for you for three reasons. You see how they actually work, not how they present. You get something useful even if you do not continue. And the price of finding out is capped and known in advance. A [teardown](/teardown) is the version of this I offer.

## How to run the free call well

A few habits get you much more out of the hour.

- Send a short note beforehand: what the company does, stage, team size, and the one problem you most want to solve.
- Bring the real problem, not the polished one. "We do not trust our agency's estimates" is more useful than "we want to improve velocity."
- Ask them what they would do first, and what they would not do.
- Ask how they price, and what is out of scope.
- Ask who else they are working with now, roughly, so you understand their availability.

And at the end, decide. Either agree a paid next step, or thank them and move on. Leaving it open, and coming back months later for another free hour, is the pattern that makes advisors stop taking first calls.

## FAQ

### Should a fractional CTO charge for the first meeting?

Most do not, and one free conversation to decide fit is normal. Charging for a first call is not wrong, especially for very experienced advisors, but it should be clear in advance.

### How long should a free consultation with a fractional CTO be?

Usually 30 to 60 minutes. Long enough to understand the problem and decide whether to work together, not long enough to solve it.

### Is it rude to ask for advice on the first call?

No. Ask real questions. A good advisor will give you a useful view of the problem. Just do not expect a detailed plan or a review of your code for free.

### What if I am not sure I need a fractional CTO at all?

Say so on the call. A good advisor will tell you honestly whether a fractional engagement, a one-off review, or a different hire fits better. The [pricing page](/pricing) shows the options, and you can [book a call](/book-a-call) to start that conversation.
