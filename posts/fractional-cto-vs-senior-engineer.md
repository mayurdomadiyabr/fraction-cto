If you have budget for exactly one more senior person and you are stuck between a fractional CTO and a senior engineer, start here: they cost roughly the same but buy completely different things. A fractional CTO buys decisions and direction a few days a week. A senior engineer buys code and throughput five days a week. Picking the wrong one is not a small mistake. It is the difference between shipping the wrong thing fast and shipping the right thing at a sane pace.

## The two things look similar on a spreadsheet and are not

A fractional CTO in 2026 runs roughly $6,000 to $15,000 a month for one to two days a week. A senior engineer in the US, fully loaded with payroll taxes, benefits, and equity, lands somewhere between $180,000 and $260,000 a year, which is $15,000 to $22,000 a month. So on the surface you are choosing between two line items of a similar size.

They are not interchangeable. The engineer writes and ships code every day. That is their job and a good one will produce a lot of it. But the engineer does not decide what to build, does not own the architecture bet, does not tell you when the thing you asked for is a mistake, and does not sit across from an investor's technical diligence and defend your stack. A fractional CTO does all of that and writes very little code, because writing code is not where their leverage is.

The mistake I see most often is a non-technical founder who hires a strong senior engineer expecting them to also be the technical leader. Some can. Most cannot, and it is unfair to expect it. Making the roadmap, saying no to the founder, sequencing technical debt, and choosing vendors is a separate skill from building well, and a senior IC has usually spent their career deliberately avoiding it.

## What each one actually buys

A senior engineer is the right spend when you already know what to build and how, and you simply need more hands that need little supervision. If you have a working architecture, a clear roadmap, and a technical founder or lead who can review the work, another senior engineer is often the highest-return hire you can make. Every dollar goes into output.

A fractional CTO is the right spend when the expensive risk is a decision, not a shortage of hands. Pre-seed and seed founders rarely have a code-throughput problem. They have a "we are about to spend six months building the wrong thing" problem, or a "the agency is quoting three times what this should cost and I cannot tell" problem, or a "an investor asked about our architecture and I froze" problem. None of those are solved by one more engineer. They are solved by senior judgment applied a couple of days a week, which is exactly the shape of a fractional engagement. This is the same logic behind [why judgment, not hours, is what you are paying for](/post-paying-for-judgment).

## How to tell which you need right now

Ask what would go wrong first if you did nothing. If the honest answer is "we have a clear plan and we just cannot ship it fast enough," hire the engineer. If the answer is "we are not sure the plan is right, or nobody here can vet the technical work," you need leadership before you need throughput, and adding an engineer only helps you build the wrong thing faster.

There is a sequencing point too. A fractional CTO often makes your eventual engineer hire cheaper and better, because they will define the role, screen the candidates, and set up the codebase so a new engineer is productive in week one instead of month two. Doing it in that order tends to protect your runway. If you want to see how the leadership spend fits against a full-time hire, the [comparison of fractional versus full-time technical leadership](/comparison) lays out the trade.

## The both-at-once trap

Founders with a little more room sometimes ask whether they should hire both. Occasionally yes, but not as a default. If you cannot yet articulate what the engineer would build for the next quarter, hiring them alongside a fractional CTO just means you are paying someone to wait for a plan. Get the plan first. The cleanest early structure I see work is a fractional CTO who sets direction and owns the technical decisions, plus one or two builders underneath them, hired only once the roadmap is real. If your budget only stretches to one seat this quarter, buy the judgment and let it tell you when to buy the hands.

## FAQ

### Is a fractional CTO cheaper than a senior engineer?
Per month they are often similar or the fractional CTO is slightly cheaper, because you are buying one to two days a week rather than five. But you are buying different things, so price parity is a coincidence, not the reason to choose. See the [honest pricing ranges by stage](/pricing) for what to expect.

### Can a senior engineer also be my technical leader?
Sometimes, but do not assume it. Leadership, roadmap ownership, and saying no to the founder are separate skills from building well, and many strong ICs neither want nor have them. If you need both, be explicit that you are hiring for leadership, and pay and screen accordingly.

### What if I can only afford one hire this quarter?
Spend on the thing that removes the biggest risk. If the risk is building the wrong product or being unable to vet technical work, buy leadership first. It usually makes the later engineer hire faster and cheaper. If you already have a vetted plan and a reviewer, buy the engineer.

### When should I add engineers under a fractional CTO?
Once the roadmap is concrete enough that an engineer has clear, reviewable work for at least a quarter. Adding builders before the plan exists means paying people to wait. If you want a second opinion on that timing, [book a call](/book-a-call) and walk through where you actually are.
