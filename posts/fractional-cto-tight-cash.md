The round is taking longer than you planned, the runway math got tight, and the fractional CTO retainer is one of the biggest discretionary lines you have. The instinct is to cut it. Before you do, understand what you are actually buying with that spend, because cutting senior technical judgment right when the company is fragile is how a tight month becomes a fatal quarter.

I have sat on both sides of this conversation. Founders assume the only options are pay in full or cancel. There is a lot of room between those two, and the operators worth keeping will work with you on it rather than watch you make a bad binary choice.

## Cut hours before you cut the person

The first move is almost never to end the engagement. It is to shrink it. A fractional CTO is already a variable-cost seat, and that variability is the whole point. You can drop from an intensive retainer to a lighter one, from every-week to every-other-week, from hands-on delivery to advisory-and-review. You keep the judgment in the room while the cash burn drops by a real fraction.

This is where the difference between paying for hours and paying for judgment shows up. If you have been treating the engagement as a bucket of hours, cutting it feels like losing throughput. If you understand [what senior engineering judgment is actually worth](/post-paying-for-judgment), you can cut the hands-on hours and keep the part that stops you making a 200,000-dollar mistake, which is the part you cannot afford to lose in a crunch.

## Scope down to the one thing that matters

A tight quarter is a forcing function. Ask the direct question: if this person can only do one thing for us this month, what is it. Usually there is a single decision or risk that dwarfs the rest, the architecture call that gates the raise, the vendor contract about to auto-renew, the hire you are about to make wrong. Point the reduced engagement at that and let the rest wait.

This is the opposite problem from the usual one, where the work quietly expands until the fractional seat looks full-time. Here you are deliberately contracting it. The same discipline applies in reverse, and it helps to have already read [how to scope the engagement so it does not creep](/post-scoping-fractional-engagement), because the crunch is when you cash in that clarity. A well-scoped engagement is easy to shrink to its core. A vague one is not, which is its own lesson for next time.

## The levers beyond cutting hours

If the constraint is genuinely temporary rather than structural, there are ways to bridge it without losing the person.

Shift to milestone or deferred billing for a defined window. Instead of a flat monthly retainer, tie a portion of the payment to a specific deliverable or to the close of the round. Good operators will sometimes carry a short deferral for a client they believe in, especially if the relationship has been solid. Do not assume it, but ask.

Bridge a slice with equity, carefully. When cash is the binding constraint, converting part of the cash rate to a small equity grant is a legitimate move, and it is common at the earliest stages. Keep it capped and keep real cash in the deal; the failure mode is going equity-heavy out of panic and regretting the cap table later. I wrote the full version of this trade in [equity versus cash for a fractional CTO](/post-fractional-cto-equity-vs-cash), and the short rule is that equity bridges a gap, it does not replace the salary.

Pause with a clean restart clause. If you truly cannot spend anything for a month or two, a formal pause with a named restart date and preserved terms beats a hard cancellation. You are not paying, but you have not lost the person or reset the relationship to zero. The right operator would rather pause and return than be fired and re-recruited.

## What not to do

Do not cancel silently and try to run without the seat during your most fragile stretch. The months around a delayed raise are exactly when a bad architecture call or a rushed hire does the most damage, and that is the worst possible time to have no senior technical read in the room.

Do not renegotiate by disappearing. Tell the person the real constraint. Operators respond well to honesty about runway and badly to being ghosted on an invoice, and you may want them back in three months. And do not let the crunch push you into an equity-only panic deal you will still be paying for on the cap table two rounds from now. If you want to talk through which lever fits your situation, [book a call](/book-a-call) and bring your actual runway number.

## FAQ

### Should I cancel my fractional CTO to save runway?
Rarely as a first move. Shrink the engagement first, fewer hours, lighter cadence, or advisory-only, so you keep the senior judgment during your most fragile stretch while cutting most of the cash burn. Cancel only if the need itself has genuinely gone away.

### Can I pay a fractional CTO partly in equity when cash is tight?
Yes, at the earliest stages, as a capped bridge with real cash still in the deal. Do not flip to equity-only out of panic; that is a cap-table decision you will feel at the next round. Treat equity as filling a gap, not replacing the rate.

### What if I cannot spend anything for a month or two?
Propose a formal pause with a named restart date and preserved terms rather than a hard cancellation. You stop paying without losing the person or resetting the relationship, and the right operator would rather pause and return than be re-recruited later.

### How do I decide what the reduced engagement should focus on?
Ask what the single most valuable thing the person can do this month is, usually the decision or risk that dwarfs everything else, and point the smaller engagement at that. A crunch is a forcing function for the prioritization you should have been doing anyway.
