If a senior engineer candidate asks for double-trigger acceleration, it is usually a reasonable request, and you can often say yes in a limited form. Double-trigger means some or all of their unvested equity vests early only if two things happen: the company is acquired, and then they are let go without cause, or quit for a defined good reason, within a set window after the deal. For a key early hire, a common middle ground is twelve months of extra vesting, or full acceleration, if they are terminated within twelve months after a sale.

Founders often hear "acceleration" and assume it will scare off buyers. That concern is real for single-trigger, which vests on the sale alone. Double-trigger is different, and it is worth understanding before you answer.

## What the candidate is actually protecting against

Picture the scenario from their side. They join at seed, take a below-market salary for a four-year grant, and two years in you sell the company. The acquirer keeps the product, decides it does not need your engineering team, and lets them go a month after closing. Without acceleration, half their grant simply disappears, and the people who decided to let them go are the ones who just bought the company.

Senior engineers who have been through an acquisition before ask for this because they have seen it happen. It is a sign of experience, not of bad faith. The candidate is not asking to be paid for a sale. They are asking not to be punished by one.

## Single-trigger vs double-trigger

- **Single-trigger:** unvested equity accelerates when the company is sold, whether or not the person stays. Buyers dislike it because the people they want to keep are suddenly fully vested and have less reason to stay. It is rare for employees and uncommon even for founders.
- **Double-trigger:** acceleration needs the sale plus a qualifying termination within a window after it. If the buyer keeps the person and treats them fairly, nothing accelerates. Buyers accept this much more readily, because it only costs them if they choose to let the person go.

Public explainers from startup law firms such as [Cooley GO](https://www.cooleygo.com/what-is-double-trigger-acceleration-and-how-does-it-work/) describe the same structure. The details that matter are the definitions, the window and the amount.

## The four terms you actually negotiate

### How much accelerates

Full acceleration of all unvested equity is the strongest form. Partial is more common for non-founders: for example, twelve months of additional vesting, or 50 percent of what is unvested. For a seed-stage senior hire, twelve months of extra vesting is a fair default. Full acceleration is something I would reserve for a very small number of people who are close to founder-level in impact.

### How long the window lasts

The second trigger usually has to happen within a set period after closing. Twelve months is a common window; some agreements use eighteen or twenty-four. Some also include a short period before closing, so a buyer cannot ask you to let someone go a week before the deal to avoid the clause.

### What counts as termination

"Without cause" needs a definition of cause. Keep it to real misconduct, not "the buyer decided to restructure". "Good reason" lets the person resign and still get acceleration if the job they were promised disappears: a material pay cut, a large reduction in responsibilities, or a forced relocation far away. Without a good-reason clause, a buyer can make the role unpleasant enough that the person quits, and the protection does nothing.

### What happens if the buyer does not assume the equity

If the acquirer cancels unvested awards instead of carrying them over, there may be nothing left to accelerate later. Better agreements say that unassumed unvested equity vests at closing. Ask your lawyer to check what your equity plan says about this, because the plan usually controls.

## Will this hurt a future sale?

For one or two key hires on double-trigger with partial acceleration, almost never in a meaningful way. Buyers expect it for senior early employees. What does cause friction:

- Single-trigger acceleration spread across the team.
- Double-trigger with full acceleration for everyone, which makes letting anyone go very expensive.
- Loose definitions of good reason that let people claim it after any reorganization.

Buyers price retention. If the acceleration terms are narrow and the people are good, the buyer usually wants to keep them anyway, and the clause never fires. ## How to answer the candidate

Do not answer with a reflexive no, and do not agree to whatever is in their draft. A good response:

1. Thank them for raising it, and say plainly that you think the concern is fair.
2. Offer double-trigger with a defined amount, usually twelve months of additional vesting, and a twelve-month post-closing window.
3. Agree to a narrow definition of cause and a reasonable good-reason clause.
4. Put it in the option grant or offer letter properly, drafted by your company lawyer, not as a line in an email.
5. Decide whether you would offer the same to the next senior hire. Precedent matters on a small team, and your first offer becomes the template.

If the candidate is also weighing salary and equity size, keep acceleration as a separate conversation. It is protection against a specific scenario, not compensation, and mixing the two makes the negotiation confusing. For the size of the grant itself, see [what to pay your first engineer](/post-first-engineer-compensation), and if a competing offer is in play, [the counteroffer post](/post-win-senior-engineer-offer) covers that side.

## When I would say no

- The person is an early-career engineer and the role is not central. Acceleration is for people whose departure after a sale would genuinely be unfair given what they gave up.
- They want single-trigger. I would explain why buyers dislike it and offer double-trigger instead.
- They want full acceleration with a very broad good-reason definition. Narrow it.

If you are making a senior offer and are not sure the terms are sensible, an outside technical leader who has been through hiring and acquisitions can tell you quickly. That is a normal part of a fractional engagement; see [pricing](/pricing) or [book a call](/book-a-call).

This is not legal or tax advice. Equity terms interact with your plan documents, tax rules and future deal terms, so have your startup lawyer draft the language.

## FAQ

### Is double-trigger acceleration standard for startup employees?

It is common for founders and senior executives, and increasingly asked for by senior early engineers. It is not automatic for every employee. Many companies offer it only to a handful of key hires, often in partial form.

### What is a typical double-trigger acceleration term for a senior engineer?

A common shape at seed stage is twelve months of additional vesting if the person is terminated without cause, or resigns for good reason, within twelve months after a change of control. Full acceleration is less common for non-founders.

### Does double-trigger acceleration scare acquirers?

Usually not when it is limited to a few people with narrow definitions. Buyers object much more to single-trigger acceleration, or to full double-trigger acceleration across the whole team.

### Can we add acceleration after the person has joined?

Yes, the board can amend an existing grant. It is simpler and cleaner to agree it at offer time, before anyone is negotiating under the pressure of a pending deal.
