Your fractional CTO is remote, the engagement is working, and someone suggests they fly in for a couple of days. Maybe it is an offsite, maybe a board meeting, maybe the team has started to feel like it is reporting to a Slack account. Then the practical questions arrive: who pays for the flight, is the day billed, and is the trip worth it at all?

Short version: travel is a pass-through expense you pay at cost with a cap agreed in advance, onsite days are billed like any other day against the retainer, and most engagements need one or two visits a year, timed to specific moments, rather than a standing monthly trip.

## Who pays for travel

The client pays. This is the near-universal convention for consulting work, and fractional CTOs follow it. The variations are in the details, and they are worth writing into the contract before the first trip, not after the first receipt.

What the clause should cover:

- **Pre-approval.** No trip happens without the founder saying yes to it in writing, including an estimated cost. A Slack message is fine.
- **At cost, no markup.** Flights, hotel and ground transport are reimbursed at what they actually cost, with receipts. A markup on travel is unusual and worth questioning. Our post on [pass-through costs on a fractional CTO's team](/post-fractional-cto-team-pass-through-cost) covers why markups on anything passed through deserve scrutiny.
- **A class of travel.** Economy for domestic flights is the common default. For long-haul, some engagements allow premium economy over a set number of hours. Decide it once.
- **Meals.** Either actual cost with receipts or a flat daily allowance. A flat daily rate is simpler and avoids arguing over dinner receipts.
- **A per-trip or annual cap.** Something like "travel not to exceed $2,500 per trip without written approval" keeps a surprise off your card.

If the fractional CTO also bills tools or software, the same at-cost principle applies; [what a fractional CTO should expense](/post-fractional-cto-tool-expenses) walks through the full list.

## Are travel days billed?

This is where engagements quietly disagree, so settle it up front.

**Onsite working days count as working days.** If your retainer covers two days a week and they spend two days in your office, those are the two days. They do not stack on top unless you agreed extra time.

**Travel time itself is the variable.** Three common arrangements:

1. **Travel time not billed.** Common for short domestic trips. The fractional CTO absorbs a few hours of flying as a cost of doing business.
2. **Travel billed at half rate.** Common in older consulting contracts for longer trips, on the logic that they can do some work on a plane but not their best work.
3. **A travel day billed as a working day.** Fair for long-haul trips that consume a full day each way. A US to Europe or Europe to India trip is two lost days for the person travelling.

None of these is wrong. What is wrong is discovering which one applies when the invoice shows up. If your engagement is a monthly retainer rather than a day rate, the simplest rule is that onsite days come out of the retainer and travel time is not billed separately unless it is long-haul. More on how retainers are structured in [what a fractional CTO retainer includes](/post-fractional-cto-retainer-includes).

## What a trip actually costs

An illustrative domestic US trip, two nights, no markup:

- Round-trip economy flight: $350 to $600
- Two nights in a mid-range hotel: $350 to $500
- Ground transport and meals: $200 to $300

Call it $900 to $1,400 all in. If travel time is not billed, that is the whole incremental cost, because the onsite days themselves were already inside the retainer.

A long-haul trip is a different number. Flights alone can run $1,200 to $2,500 in economy, and if each travel day is billed as a working day, you are adding two days of fees on top. For a fractional CTO at $1,500 a day, a long-haul visit can cost $5,000 to $7,000 once fees and expenses are added up. That is not a reason to refuse it. It is a reason to make sure the trip does something remote work cannot.

## When a visit is worth it

Most of the work a fractional CTO does is fine remote: architecture reviews, hiring loops, vendor calls, one-to-ones, written decisions. Travel earns its cost at a short list of moments.

**The first two weeks.** An early visit compresses the context-gathering phase. Sitting with the team for two days surfaces things that take a month to learn over video: who actually makes decisions, what the codebase feels like to work in, which tensions are real.

**A hard team conversation.** Restructuring, letting someone go, a lead who is about to quit. These go better in person, and a fractional CTO who has been in the room with the team has more standing to hold them.

**Planning sessions.** A half-day roadmap or architecture session with the whole team is noticeably better face to face. If you run a quarterly or half-yearly planning cycle, that is a natural visit.

**A board meeting or diligence session.** When the fractional CTO is presenting to investors or an acquirer, being in the room adds credibility. The [three-page tech memo for investors](/post-tech-memo-investors) is a good example of the material they would be walking through.

What does not justify a trip: routine weekly work, status updates, or a vague sense that the team should see them more. If the team feels disconnected, the fix is usually more consistent remote presence, a regular slot every week, not a quarterly flight.

## A rule of thumb for frequency

For a typical seed or Series A engagement at one to two days a week:

- **One visit in the first month**, for onboarding.
- **One or two visits a year after that**, tied to planning or a specific event.
- **An extra visit only for a named reason**, agreed in advance.

If a fractional CTO is proposing monthly trips, ask what each one is for. If the answer is "presence," the engagement may be drifting toward a full-time role in everything but title, which is a different conversation. [Signs you have outgrown fractional](/post-outgrown-fractional-cto) covers that.

## What to put in the contract

A clean travel clause has five lines: travel requires written pre-approval; reimbursed at actual cost with receipts and no markup; economy class unless otherwise agreed; travel time not billed for trips under a set number of hours, and billed at an agreed rate above that; and a per-trip cap above which a second approval is needed.

That is enough to prevent every travel dispute I have seen in an engagement.

## Questions founders ask

### Should a local fractional CTO charge for coming to the office?

Usually not, beyond normal commuting. If they live in your city, office days are just working days. Parking or a train fare is sometimes reimbursed, and that is a small thing to agree once.

### Can I insist on regular onsite days in the contract?

You can, and some founders do when the team is fully in-office. Write the cadence in explicitly, for example one day a fortnight, and expect it to narrow the pool of people you can hire, since it effectively requires them to be local.

### Is it cheaper to hire someone local?

Sometimes. A local fractional CTO removes travel costs and makes in-person work easy. But the right person for your stage and problem matters far more than a few thousand dollars a year in flights.
