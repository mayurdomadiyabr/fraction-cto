A founder wiring $10,000 a month to a fractional CTO for the first time almost always asks the same nervous question a few weeks in: what am I actually getting for this? There is no timesheet, no ticket queue, no obvious pile of output. That discomfort is normal, and the answer is worth spelling out, because a retainer is a strange thing to buy if nobody has told you what is inside it.

I run these engagements, so let me tell you what a retainer is really for, where founders think the value is versus where it actually is, and how to check every month that you are getting your money's worth.

## What is inside the fee

A fractional CTO retainer buys three things: availability, ownership, and judgment. The order matters.

Availability means you can reach me without it being an event. The 6am worry about your architecture, the "is this agency quote fair," the gut-check before you make an offer to an engineer. On a flat fee you ask immediately, which is the entire point. The most valuable thing I do in a given month might take fifteen minutes, and a clock would make us both pretend it did not happen. That is the same reasoning behind [why I don't bill by the hour](/post-no-hourly).

Ownership means someone other than you is accountable for the technology going well. Not "advised on," owned. That covers the roadmap being sane, the team shipping the right things, the vendors being managed, the security posture not being a liability, and the board getting a straight technical story. When a decision has to be made and you do not know the answer, it stops with me instead of stopping with you.

Judgment is the part you cannot see and are actually paying for. Knowing that the rewrite everyone wants is a trap. Knowing which piece of technical debt to fix now and which to ignore for a year. Knowing that a candidate who interviews beautifully will not survive your first real incident. That is the compounding value, and it is invisible precisely because good judgment prevents problems rather than solving visible ones.

## What the market actually charges for it

Concrete numbers, because "it depends" is not useful when you are budgeting.

Monthly retainers for a fractional CTO run roughly $5,000 to $18,000, with the median serious engagement landing around $8,000 to $12,000 a month for something like 15 to 20 hours a week of involvement. North American rates sit at the high end, $10,000 to $16,000; Western Europe runs a little lower. That is typically 60 to 75 percent less than the fully loaded cost of a full-time CTO once you count salary, equity, benefits, and payroll tax.

A detail most founders miss: the retainer usually carries a 10 to 20 percent discount versus the same person's hourly rate. That is not generosity. The provider gets guaranteed income and you get guaranteed availability, and both sides pay a little for that certainty. If someone quotes you a retainer that works out to more per hour than their hourly rate, ask why.

## What is not inside the fee

This is where disappointment comes from, so be clear up front.

A fractional CTO retainer is leadership, not labor. It does not usually include 40 hours a week of hands-on coding. If your actual need is "build the product," you need engineers, and part of the retainer's job is to help you hire and manage them. Buying a fractional CTO and expecting a full-time developer's output is the most common mismatch I see, and it ends badly for both sides.

It also does not include unlimited scope. Fifteen to twenty hours a week is real but finite. During a fundraise, a major architecture decision, or a hiring sprint, that time gets consumed fast. A good engagement flexes for those peaks and settles back down, but nobody is on call 24 hours a day for a fractional fee.

And it is not a body to blame when the team misses. Ownership is shared. You still have to make the calls the CTO frames for you, fund the plan, and let them manage the people.

## How to check you are getting your money's worth

Do not measure a retainer by hours logged. Measure it by whether the technology stopped being the thing you lie awake about. A few concrete signals, month to month:

Can you now brief your board or an investor on the technical side in plain language without panic? Are decisions getting made instead of waiting on you? Did the last agency invoice or vendor renewal get challenged by someone who knew what they were reading? Is the roadmap shorter and more honest than it was? Those are the outputs. If after a couple of months you cannot point to any of them, the engagement is scoped wrong or the person is wrong, and you should say so out loud.

The retainer is cancellable month to month for exactly this reason. The only way it survives is by being obviously worth more than it costs, every single month.

### FAQ

### Does a retainer include writing production code?

Usually not as the main activity. Some fractional CTOs will prototype or unblock a hard technical problem hands-on, but the retainer is priced as senior leadership time, not full-time development. If you need sustained coding output, budget for engineers separately and use the CTO to hire and direct them.

### What happens to hours I do not use in a slow month?

Most retainers do not track or roll over hours, because you are buying availability and ownership, not a bucket of time. A quiet month is not wasted money; it is the months where one fifteen-minute call saves you a $100,000 mistake that pay for the quiet ones.

### How is a retainer different from just paying hourly?

Hourly makes you ration access and hide small questions, which are often the important ones. A flat retainer removes that friction and aligns the incentive: the provider keeps the engagement only by being worth it. See [why I don't bill by the hour](/post-no-hourly) for the full argument.

### How do I know if the fee is fair for my stage?

Compare it to the loaded cost of the full-time hire you are avoiding and to the market bands above. If you want a second opinion on a specific quote or scope, [book a call](/book-a-call) and I will tell you straight whether it is priced right.
