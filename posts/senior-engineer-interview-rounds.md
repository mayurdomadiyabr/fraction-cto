For a senior engineer at a seed-stage startup, three interview stages after an initial screen is enough: a deep technical conversation about their past work, a practical exercise close to your real problems, and a meeting with the founders about the role and the company. Every stage should test something the others do not. If you cannot say what a round adds, cut it. A long loop does not make the decision safer; it mostly filters out the candidates who have other options.

I see founders add rounds for one reason: fear. Nobody on the team can fully judge a senior engineer, so they add another conversation, then another, hoping confidence will appear. It rarely does. What appears instead is a six-week process that the best candidate quits in week three.

## Why more rounds do not mean a better decision

Each extra round has a cost you can see and a cost you cannot.

The visible cost is time. Five interviewers at an hour each, plus prep and debrief, is a real chunk of a small team's week. Multiply by the number of candidates and the search starts competing with the roadmap.

The invisible cost is who drops out. Strong senior engineers usually have several processes running. Each week your process takes is a week another company can make an offer. The candidates who tolerate a seven-round loop are disproportionately the ones with fewer alternatives. Recruiting firms and hiring blogs report the same pattern, that longer loops lose more strong candidates, though most of those figures come from vendors rather than controlled studies, so treat them as directional.

There is also a quieter problem. After four or five conversations, interviewers start judging the same things again with slightly different questions. You do not gain new information; you gain more chances for one person to veto on a feeling.

## A three-stage loop that covers what matters

Here is the loop I recommend to most founders hiring their first or second senior engineer. It assumes a short screen first, a 20 to 30 minute call to check basics like location, salary range, and start date.

### Stage 1: the past-work deep dive (60 to 90 minutes)

Ask the candidate to walk you through one or two systems they built or significantly changed. Then go deep. Why that database? What broke in production? What would they do differently? What did they push back on, and did they win?

This round tests judgment and depth more than anything else. Someone who designed a system can explain the tradeoffs and the failures. Someone who was near it describes the architecture diagram. I wrote more about the specific questions in [screening a senior engineer for judgment](/post-screen-engineer-for-judgment).

If nobody on your team is technical, this is the stage where you most need outside help. A founder can still run it, and I covered how in [interviewing an engineer when you cannot read code](/post-interview-engineer-non-technical), but having a senior engineer in the room changes the depth you get.

### Stage 2: a practical exercise (2 to 4 hours, paid if long)

Give them a problem shaped like your real work: review a small pull request with planted issues, extend a small codebase, or design a feature you actually need with them talking through choices. Keep it bounded. Pay for anything over a couple of hours.

This round tests whether they can do the work in your context. Avoid puzzle questions and generic algorithm tests; they measure interview practice, not the job. If take-homes have stopped giving you signal, see [what to do instead of the take-home](/post-engineer-work-sample-test).

### Stage 3: founders and role fit (45 to 60 minutes)

This is the conversation about the job itself: what the first six months look like, who they report to, how decisions get made, what the company can and cannot pay. It is also where the candidate interviews you. Expect sharp questions about runway and priorities, and treat good questions as a positive signal.

This round tests mutual fit and sells the role. Founders often skip the selling, assuming the candidate is already convinced. They are not.

### What about references?

References are not a round, but they are part of the loop. Do them after stage 3 for your top candidate, before the offer. Two or three calls, focused on specific behavior. The questions that work are in [reference checks that tell you something](/post-engineer-reference-checks).

## Rounds worth cutting

If your current process is longer than this, here is what I usually remove:

- **A second technical round that repeats the first.** If stage 1 and the exercise both test system design, keep one.
- **The "team fit" panel with the whole company.** At five people, everyone meeting the candidate is fine as a casual lunch. As a scored round, it becomes a popularity vote.
- **An investor or advisor interview.** Useful for a CTO search. For a senior engineer, it signals the founder does not trust their own judgment.
- **A final founder call that re-asks stage 3.** If you need to sell harder, do it in the offer conversation.

## How fast the whole loop should run

Aim for two to three weeks from first screen to offer for a senior engineer. That means scheduling stages within a few days of each other and debriefing within 24 hours of each round. The single biggest delay I see is not the number of rounds; it is the gap between them, while founders find time on their calendars.

A simple discipline helps: before the first screen, block the slots for every stage for the next two weeks. If a strong candidate appears, the time is already there. I covered the full timeline, including sourcing, in [how long hiring your first engineer should take](/post-first-engineer-hiring-timeline).

## When a longer loop is justified

There are two cases where I would add a stage.

The first is when the role is effectively your technical leader: the person who will make architecture decisions with nobody to check them. A paid trial week, or a session with an outside senior engineer, is worth the extra time. A short [paid trial](/post-paid-trial-first-engineer) can replace part of the loop rather than adding to it.

The second is when a stage produced a split signal. One extra focused conversation on the specific doubt is better than starting the loop over. Name the doubt, test only that, and decide.

## FAQ

### Is three rounds enough to avoid a bad senior hire?

Three well-designed rounds catch more problems than six repetitive ones, because each tests something different. The bigger risk is not round count but having nobody qualified to judge the technical stages. Fix that first.

### Should the practical exercise be paid?

If it takes more than about two hours, yes. Paying signals respect for the candidate's time and lets you ask for something closer to real work. A few hundred dollars is small compared to the cost of a wrong hire.

### What if the candidate asks to skip the exercise?

Some very senior engineers decline take-homes. Offer an alternative that gives the same signal, such as a live review of a real pull request or a design session, rather than dropping the stage entirely.

### How quickly should we give feedback after each round?

Within one or two business days. Fast feedback keeps strong candidates in your process and forces your team to decide while the conversation is fresh.
