Should you use a recruiter for your first engineer?

Jul 20265 min readby The founder of Fraction

A founder asked me last month whether he should hand his first engineering hire to a contingency recruiter or keep running the search himself. He had a term sheet, a six-month runway plan, and no engineering network. The recruiter quoted 22 percent of first-year salary. On the $180,000 role he was hiring for, that is roughly $40,000, or about a month of his post-raise burn. The question is not whether recruiters are good or bad. It is whether that fee buys you something you cannot get another way for your first hire specifically.

What a recruiter actually sells you

A contingency recruiter is paid only when you hire, and the market rate for engineering roles in 2026 sits between 20 and 25 percent of first-year base salary. On a $175,000 senior engineer that is $35,000 to $44,000. Retained search, where you pay in installments regardless of outcome, runs higher, 25 to 33 percent, and is rare below the VP level. There are cheaper models - flat-fee outsourced recruiting around $3,000 to $6,000 per hire, referral bonuses of a few thousand dollars - but the default a founder gets pitched is contingency.

What you are buying at that price is not access to secret candidates. It is throughput. A good recruiter runs the top of the funnel: sourcing, first-touch outreach, scheduling, chasing, and keeping five conversations warm at once so one dropout does not reset your search. For a founder doing this between customer calls and a board update, that throughput is real. The failure mode is that a contingency recruiter is paid to close any hire, not the right one, and their incentive points at speed, not fit.

The part they cannot do for you

The recruiter can fill your pipeline. They cannot tell you whether the person in front of you has the judgment your specific stage needs, because they do not carry your consequences and usually cannot evaluate senior engineering work themselves. For your first engineer that gap matters more than for your tenth, because there is no engineer inside the company to run the technical bar. If you outsource sourcing and you have no one to vet the output, you have outsourced the easy half and kept the hard half unsupported. I wrote more about that gap in who vets your engineer when you have no engineers.

When the fee is worth it, and when it is not

Use a few plain tests before you sign anything.

Reasons it is worth paying

You have real money and no time. Post-raise, with a burn plan that assumes the hire lands this quarter, $40,000 to compress a three-month search to six weeks can be the cheapest line item you have. Another good reason is a domain you cannot source yourself - hard security, ML infrastructure, embedded systems - where the strong candidates are not on the job boards you already know. A third is that you have tried for six weeks on your own, your pipeline is dry, and the empty seat now costs you more in stalled roadmap than the fee costs you in cash.

Reasons to keep it in-house

Your first engineer is often someone you already half-know: a former colleague, a strong contractor, a warm intro from an investor. Those people rarely come through a contingency desk, and if they do, you pay a fee on a candidate you would have found for free. Early engineers also weigh the founder relationship heavily, and a recruiter-mediated first contact can read as less personal than a founder reaching out directly about the mission. If your own network has three plausible names in it, work those first. The fee is only worth paying once your own funnel is genuinely empty.

How to hire a recruiter without getting hurt

If you do use one, structure the deal so their incentive does not quietly run your process.

Negotiate the rate. Twenty percent is a starting number, not a fixed one. Multiple upcoming roles, a lower salary band, or a longer relationship all pull it toward 15 to 17 percent. Ask for a longer replacement guarantee - 90 days is common, push for 180 - so a hire who leaves in month four is refilled free. Keep the final technical evaluation yours: bring in a trusted senior engineer or a fractional lead to run the judgment screen, and make clear the recruiter's job ends at a qualified, genuinely interested candidate. And write the role down properly before you engage anyone, because a vague brief produces a vague pipeline. If you have not fixed what you are actually hiring for, start there and with your compensation band; I covered the money side in what to pay your first engineer and the search itself in where to actually find your first senior engineer.

FAQ

Is a contingency recruiter cheaper than retained for a first hire?

Upfront, yes - you pay nothing until a hire lands, which suits a startup guarding runway. Retained search costs 25 to 33 percent and is paid in stages regardless of outcome, which almost never makes sense below a VP or executive role. For a first individual-contributor engineer, contingency or in-house sourcing are the realistic options.

Can I use a recruiter and still control the bar?

Yes, and you should. Let them own sourcing and scheduling, but keep the technical and judgment evaluation in-house or with a fractional CTO. The recruiter's incentive is to close; your job is to make sure the person they close is right for your stage, not just available this month.

What if I have no engineering network at all?

Then a recruiter or a fractional technical partner is buying you the network you do not have. Just do not pair outsourced sourcing with no one to vet the result. Pair the pipeline with someone who can judge the work before you make an offer.

How long should the search take with a recruiter?

A well-run engineering search with an engaged recruiter typically fills in six to ten weeks. If you are past twelve weeks with a full pipeline and no offer, the problem is usually the bar or the pitch, not the sourcing - and no fee fixes that.
