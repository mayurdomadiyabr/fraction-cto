Your roadmap slipped right before the board meeting

Nov 20266 min readby The founder of Fraction

The launch you promised the board for this quarter is not going to happen. You found out three weeks ago, the board meeting is in nine days, and the deck still shows the old date. Most founders in this spot do one of two things: they quietly move the date on a slide and hope nobody asks, or they open the meeting with a long apology. Both cost more trust than the slip itself.

The short answer: tell your lead board member before the meeting, present the slip as one slide with the old date, the new date, the cause and what you changed, and ask for a decision only if you actually need one. Boards forgive late. They do not forgive finding out late.

Why the slip is rarely the real problem

A product date moving by six weeks at seed or Series A is normal. Almost every early roadmap is a guess dressed up as a plan, and experienced directors know it. What they are actually watching is whether you see problems early and whether you tell them straight.

When I sit with founders before a board meeting, the conversation is almost never about the delay. It is about the story they were planning to tell. The common versions:

  • The silent slide edit. The date moves from Q3 to Q4 with no comment. A director who keeps notes will spot it, and now the question is not "why is it late" but "what else did you move without saying".
  • The engineering blame. "The team underestimated." This puts the board in the position of judging your engineers, which is not their job, and it signals you do not own the plan.
  • The technical lecture. Ten minutes on database migrations and third-party APIs. Directors cannot evaluate it, so they hear "this is out of control".

The fix is the same in all three cases: make the slip boring, owned and brief.

Brief the lead director before the meeting

Call your lead investor or the most engaged director a few days before. Five minutes is enough: "Heads up, the integrations launch is moving from October to mid-December. Here is why, here is what we changed, and I do not need anything from the board unless you see it differently." This does two things. The person most likely to ask the hard question in the room already knows the answer, and you get a read on whether the slip matters more to them than you think. Sometimes it does, because a follow-on round or a customer renewal they care about depends on that date. Better to learn that on the phone than in the meeting.

If your board is just you and one investor, the call replaces the slide entirely. A written note afterward is still worth sending so there is a record.

The one slide that does the job

Put the slip on a single slide with four lines.

Old date, new date

State both plainly. Do not use ranges unless you genuinely have one, and if you do, say what has to be true to hit the early end.

The cause, in business terms

One or two sentences a non-technical director can repeat to their partners. "The payments provider changed its onboarding requirements in August, which added a compliance review we had not planned for" works. "Refactoring the billing service took longer than expected" does not, because it explains nothing about why.

Be honest about which kind of cause it is. Roadmaps slip for a small set of reasons: the scope grew, an outside dependency moved, the team was smaller than planned (a hire fell through, someone left), or the estimate was simply wrong. Each one has a different fix, and naming it shows you know which one you are dealing with. If the estimate was wrong, say so. That is the most common cause and the one founders most often disguise.

What you changed

This is the line directors actually read. A slip without a change is a forecast that the next date will slip too. Concrete changes look like: cut two features from the first release, moved one engineer off maintenance work for six weeks, added a weekly demo of working software so you see drift sooner, or replaced the dependency. If the change is "we will work harder", you do not have a change.

What it affects

Revenue, a customer commitment, the next raise or nothing. If a customer was promised the feature, say whether they know and how they took it. If the slip pushes a milestone your next round depends on, say that too, and say what the new milestone timing means for runway. This is where a founder earns credibility: connecting an engineering date to the numbers the board actually owns.

When you do need a decision

Most slips need no board decision. A few do: you want to spend money you did not budget (a contractor, a vendor), the slip changes the fundraising timeline, or the honest fix is cutting something a director championed. In those cases, give the board two options with a recommendation, not an open question. "Option A: ship in December with the reduced scope, no extra spend. Option B: ship the full scope in February with one contractor at roughly $25,000. We recommend A." Directors are good at choosing between options and bad at designing them in a meeting.

Stop the next surprise

The real lesson of a slip found three weeks before a board meeting is that you found it three weeks before a board meeting. Usually the signs were there earlier: a feature that kept being "almost done", a sprint review where nothing was demoable, an estimate nobody revisited after the scope changed.

Two habits catch most of this early. First, judge progress by working software you can click, not by status reports. Second, re-estimate any date-critical project every two weeks and write the current best guess next to the committed date. When those two numbers drift apart, you have your early warning, and the board conversation happens on your schedule instead of the calendar's. We cover the broader version of this in how to explain technical risk to your board, and the pattern of dates that were never credible in the first place in roadmaps that read as wishlists.

If slips keep landing on you as surprises, that is usually a leadership gap rather than an engineering one. An outside read on where the plan is actually standing is what a one-time technical teardown is for, and if you want to talk through a specific slip before your meeting, book a call.

FAQ

Should I lead the board meeting with the slip?

Not usually. Put it where it belongs in your normal flow, typically in the product or engineering section, and keep it to the one slide. Leading with it makes it the headline of the meeting. The exception is when the slip changes the fundraising plan, in which case it belongs up front with the financials.

How much detail should I give if a director pushes?

Answer the question asked, in business terms, and offer a follow-up with whoever on your team can go deeper. Do not try to teach the architecture in the room. If you cannot answer the follow-up yourself, say so and commit to a date for the answer.

What if this is the second slip on the same project?

Then the conversation changes. A second slip means the first fix did not work, and the board will reasonably ask whether the plan, the team or the scope is wrong. Come with an honest view on which one it is and a smaller, safer next milestone you are confident about.

Do I need to tell the board about slips on internal projects?

Only if they affect something the board owns: cash, a customer, a hire or the raise. An internal tooling project moving two weeks is not board material. The test is whether a director would be surprised and annoyed to learn about it later.
