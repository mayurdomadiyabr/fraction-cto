Should your founding engineer get the CTO title?

Jul 20265 min readby The founder of Fraction

Your first engineer has been with you since the beginning. They built the product, they know every corner of the system, and lately they have started asking, gently or not, for the CTO title. Or you are thinking of offering it to keep them. Either way, the question is on the table: should your founding engineer be your CTO?

It feels like a small thing, a word on an email signature. It is not. The title sets expectations you may not be able to meet later, and taking it back is one of the more painful conversations in an early company.

The title and the job are not the same

A CTO title at a five-person startup and a CTO title at a 200-person company describe very different jobs. At scale, a CTO barely codes. They set technical strategy, manage managers, own hiring, sit in front of the board, and shape partnerships. At five people, the same title usually means the best engineer who ships the most product.

Those are different roles, and the gap matters because titles ratchet in one direction. You can promote a founding engineer to CTO. Walking it back when the company grows and needs a different kind of leader is brutal, and it often ends with the person leaving.

What the person is actually asking for

When a founding engineer asks for the CTO title, they usually want one of three things, and it pays to find out which.

Sometimes they want recognition: acknowledgment that they took early risk and built the core of the company. Sometimes they want security: a signal that they will not be leapfrogged by a future outside hire. And sometimes they genuinely want the job: to lead people, own strategy, and grow into an executive.

The first two are real and worth honoring, but they do not require the CTO title. The third does, and it is the rarest. Many excellent founding engineers are superb builders who do not want to spend their days in one-on-ones and hiring loops. Handing them a management title rewards them with work they will not enjoy and may not be good at.

What happens when the title outgrows the person

The common failure is this. You give the founding engineer the CTO title at year one. The company raises, grows to fifteen engineers, and now needs someone who can build and lead an organization. Your CTO is a phenomenal engineer and a reluctant, untested manager. You have three bad options: keep them in a role they cannot fill, hire above them and create a demotion in all but name, or lose them.

I have watched this play out more than once. The kinder path is to be honest early about what the title commits you to. If the founding engineer might genuinely become the executive, invest in that deliberately: give them scoped leadership, coaching, and time to grow, and evaluate honestly whether they are on that track. If they are a builder at heart, protect that. The choice between a CTO and a VP of engineering is worth reading here, because the leadership your company needs at scale is often the VP-of-engineering job, not the CTO one, and forcing it onto a founding engineer serves nobody.

Better alternatives to the CTO title

You have more options than "CTO or nothing."

The cleanest is "Founding Engineer," a title that is increasingly well understood and that carries real weight. It signals early risk, deep ownership of core architecture, and a shaping role in the company, without promising the executive job. It ages well, because it describes what actually happened rather than a role the person may never grow into.

You can also address the underlying want directly. If it is recognition, give equity and public credit. Founding engineers earn meaningful equity, and getting the compensation and equity for a first engineer right does more for retention than a title does. If it is security about a future outside hire, be candid about the plan and where they fit in it. A straight conversation beats a title handed out to avoid one.

When the founding engineer should get the title

There is a real version where the answer is yes.

If the founding engineer clearly wants to lead, is already doing the work of setting technical direction and growing the team, and has shown they can manage people well, then formalizing it recognizes reality rather than gambling on potential. Title should follow demonstrated scope, not precede it. When someone is already functioning as the technical leader, withholding the title is its own mistake.

The related question, often lurking underneath, is whether the founder should still be holding the CTO role themselves. If you are a technical founder deciding whether to hand the title down, the signals for when the technical founder should stop being the CTO are the other half of this decision.

If you are weighing this and want an outside read on whether your founding engineer is a future executive or a builder you should protect as one, book a call. It is easier to get the title right the first time than to correct it after the company has grown around it.

Frequently asked questions

Will I lose my founding engineer if I do not give them the CTO title?

Not if you address what they actually want. If it is recognition or security, equity, credit, and an honest conversation usually satisfy it. You lose people by ignoring the ask, not by declining a specific title while meeting the real need behind it.

Is "Founding Engineer" a real title investors respect?

Yes. It has become a well-understood signal for the first few technical hires who shaped the product and took early risk. It reads as substance, not as a consolation prize, and it does not create the expectations that an early CTO title does.

What if I gave the title already and it no longer fits?

Address it directly and early, before an outside hire forces the issue. Options include growing the person into the role with real support, restructuring so they own a domain they are great at, or, if it cannot work, an honest transition. The longer it waits, the harder it gets.

Can a founding engineer become a real CTO later?

Absolutely, and many do. The point is to let the title follow demonstrated leadership rather than granting it up front and hoping. If they grow into strategy, hiring, and people management, formalizing it then is earned and stable.
