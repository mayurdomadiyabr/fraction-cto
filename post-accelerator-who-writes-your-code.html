The accelerator asks who writes your code. Answer it straight

Nov 20265 min readby The founder of Fraction

When an accelerator application asks who writes your code, answer with names, roles and ownership, not reassurance. If a freelancer, an agency or a contractor built part of the product, say so, say what they built, and say that the company owns the result. The question exists because reviewers want to know whether the team can keep building after the money arrives. A clear, honest answer helps you. A vague one costs you.

Y Combinator's application has, for years, asked a version of this: who writes code or does other technical work on your product, and whether any of it was done by a non-founder. You can see a range of real answers in this collection of applicant responses. Other accelerators and many angel investors ask the same thing in different words.

What the question is really testing

From the outside it looks like a box to tick. In practice reviewers are reading for three things.

Can this team build without help?

If the product depends on someone who is not a founder and is not committed long term, the company has a gap. That is not disqualifying. Plenty of companies start with outside developers. But the reviewer wants to see that you know it and have a plan.

Is the team being straight with us?

An application that says "we built it ourselves" when the commit history, a public portfolio or a quick conversation shows an agency did most of the work is a trust problem. Accelerators talk to many founders and they notice. Being caught overstating technical capability early tends to colour everything else in the application.

Does the company own what was built?

If outside people wrote the code, did they assign the rights to the company? Missing paperwork here is one of the most common issues in early technical diligence. Our piece on who owns your code covers why it matters more than founders expect.

How to answer in common situations

Here is how I would phrase it in the situations I see most. Adapt the wording; keep the structure: who, what, ownership, plan.

A technical founder wrote it

Simple. Name the founder, give a one-line sense of their background, and mention any help. "Priya, CTO, wrote the backend and the web app. A part-time contractor built the first iOS screens in March under an assignment agreement; Priya now maintains them."

A freelancer or agency built the first version

Say so, say what you have done since, and confirm ownership. "Our MVP was built by a three-person agency between January and April. All code is in our own repository under a contract that assigns IP to the company. Since May, Sam (co-founder) has made all changes, and we plan to hire our first full-time engineer after the batch."

What reviewers want to see is the transition: who owns the code now, who can change it, and what changes after funding. If you are not technical and still depend fully on the agency, say that too, and say what you are doing about it. Our guide on raising without a technical co-founder goes deeper on that position.

AI tools wrote a lot of it

Increasingly common, and nothing to hide. Reviewers mostly care whether a person on the team understands the code, can change it, and takes responsibility for it. "Sam built the product using AI coding tools; he reviews and tests every change and can walk through any part of the system" is a fine answer if it is true. If nobody on the team can explain how the code works, that is the real problem to solve, and it shows up quickly in an interview.

A friend helped for free

This one causes more trouble than founders expect. A friend who wrote a meaningful part of the product with no written agreement may have rights in that code. Mention them honestly and get a short assignment signed before you apply. It is usually a ten-minute conversation now and a much harder one after you raise.

What to fix before you submit

A few things are worth doing in the week before the application goes in. As a rule of thumb, they take a day or two in total.

  1. Move the code into a company-owned repository if it lives in a freelancer's or agency's account.
  2. Collect IP assignment agreements from everyone who wrote code, including former contractors. The post on IP assignment before a raise has the checklist. Get legal advice for anything unusual; this is not legal advice.
  3. Make sure a founder can deploy. If only the agency can ship to production, fix that first.
  4. Write a one-paragraph technical summary of how the product works. You will reuse it in every investor conversation.

The interview follow-up

If you get an interview, expect a follow-up on whatever you wrote. "You said the agency built the MVP. What happens if they disappear tomorrow?" Have a crisp answer: where the code is, who can deploy it, how long it would take a new engineer to get productive. Founders who answer that in thirty seconds come across as in control of their company, which is exactly what the question was designed to find out.

If your honest answer is "we would be stuck", that is worth fixing before demo day, not after. An outside technical teardown can tell you how stuck, and a fractional CTO can help you plan the handover; the pricing page explains how that works.

FAQ

Will saying an agency built our product hurt our chances?

Not on its own. Lots of funded companies started that way. What hurts is hiding it, not owning the code, or having no plan for building after the batch.

Do I need a technical co-founder to get into an accelerator?

No accelerator I know of requires one in its rules, but reviewers do weigh a team's ability to build. Without one, show how product development continues: who builds, who decides, and what you will hire.

Should I list AI coding tools as "non-founder work"?

I would mention them briefly if they did a large share of the work, framed as tools the founders used, with a founder responsible for the result. The question is about people and accountability, not which editor you use.

Can someone review our answer before we apply?

Yes. A short call is usually enough to sharpen the technical parts of an application. Book a call if you want a second opinion.
