Your agency is abroad. Can you enforce the contract?

Nov 20265 min readby The founder of Fraction

If your development agency is in another country, the contract you signed is only as strong as your ability to enforce it there. A judgment from your home court may be hard or slow to enforce abroad, so for cross-border agency work the dispute clause, especially whether disputes go to arbitration, often matters more than the rest of the contract. Before you sign, decide where a dispute would be heard and check that a decision there could actually be enforced where the agency and its assets are.

Most founders who hire offshore never think about this, and most of the time it never matters. It matters a lot in the one engagement that goes wrong: the agency stops work, keeps the code, or refuses to refund a prepayment. This post is general guidance from the vendor side of the table, not legal advice. For a real dispute, or a large contract, talk to a lawyer qualified in the relevant countries.

Why the governing law line is not boilerplate

Every agency contract has a short clause near the end saying which country's law applies and which courts or tribunal will hear disputes. Founders usually accept whatever the agency's template says. That template almost always names the agency's home country.

That is not automatically bad. But think through what it means. If the agency stops delivering and you want your prepayment back, you would be bringing a claim in a foreign legal system, in a language you may not speak, through local lawyers you do not know, against a company that knows that system well. Many founders conclude at that point that the claim is not worth pursuing, and the agency often knows that too.

Courts vs arbitration for cross-border work

There are two broad routes.

Court litigation. A court in your home country can give you a judgment, but the agency's assets are abroad. Getting a foreign court to enforce that judgment depends on treaties and local rules between the two countries, which vary widely. In some pairs of countries it works reasonably; in many it is slow, uncertain or effectively unavailable.

Arbitration. The parties agree that a private tribunal, under named rules, will decide disputes. The big advantage for cross-border deals is enforcement: the New York Convention on foreign arbitral awards has 172 contracting states, which commit to recognise and enforce awards made in other member states, subject to limited grounds for refusal. That gives an award a much more predictable path into the agency's home country than a foreign court judgment usually has.

Arbitration is not cheap or fast by default. Fees for arbitrators and institutions can be significant, which is why it suits larger contracts. For a 20,000 dollar engagement, the cost of formal arbitration can exceed what is at stake. For a 300,000 dollar build, it can be the difference between a recoverable claim and a lost one.

What I ask for before signing an offshore agency

These are practical points I raise when reviewing an agency contract. Your lawyer may suggest different ones.

  1. A named dispute route. Either a court in a specific place, or arbitration under named rules, with a seat and a language. Vague wording like "the parties will resolve disputes amicably" is not a dispute clause.
  2. A neutral or practical seat. If you cannot get your own country, a well-known arbitration seat that both sides can live with is often a reasonable middle ground.
  3. A step before formal action. A short escalation step, such as a written notice and a senior call within a set number of days, settles most disagreements without lawyers.
  4. Smaller claims handled sensibly. Some contracts allow a faster, cheaper process for claims under a set amount. Ask whether that is available under the rules you pick.
  5. IP that transfers as you pay. Code, designs and accounts should become yours with each paid invoice, not at the very end. That way a dispute over the last payment does not hold your whole product hostage. Our list of SOW red flags covers this clause in more detail.

Your real protection is operational

Honestly, the contract is the backstop. The things that actually protect founders in offshore engagements are the boring operational ones:

  • Do not prepay large amounts. Pay monthly or by milestone, in arrears where you can. Large upfront payments are the main source of unrecoverable losses; we wrote about this in agency upfront payments.
  • Own the repository and the cloud accounts from day one. If the code lives in your GitHub and the servers in your cloud account, the agency has far less leverage in a dispute.
  • Watch for early signs of trouble. Missed payroll at the agency, sudden developer rotation and slower replies often come before a collapse. Our post on an agency in financial trouble lists the signs.
  • Keep the working relationship healthy. Most offshore problems are communication problems first. Managing an offshore team covers the routines that keep small issues small.

If those four are in place, you will rarely need the dispute clause. If they are not, even a perfect clause will leave you with an expensive fight.

FAQ

Is it worth paying a lawyer to review a small agency contract?

For a few thousand dollars of work, a careful founder reading plus a checklist is often enough. Once the contract runs into six figures, or involves a large prepayment, a short review by a lawyer familiar with cross-border work is cheap insurance.

Is my country part of the New York Convention?

Most countries are. The official list of contracting states shows current members and any reservations they made.

The agency refuses to change its standard clause. What then?

Decide whether you can manage the risk operationally instead: no large prepayments, code and accounts in your name, monthly billing. If you can, the clause matters less.

Can I just sue them in my local court?

You may be able to get a judgment, but enforcing it where the agency's money is can be the hard part. Ask a lawyer how that works between your two specific countries before relying on it.
